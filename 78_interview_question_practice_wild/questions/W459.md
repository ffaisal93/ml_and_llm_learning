## Question

A behavioral round run by a partner from a non-engineering function (PM, design, sometimes data science). It probes collaboration patterns, conflict navigation, and how the candidate scopes work with cross-functional stakeholders.

Typical prompts
A time you disagreed with a PM/designer/data scientist on a major decision, and how it resolved.
A project you scoped from a vague product request into something engineerable.
A success and the trade-offs negotiated with non-engineering partners.
A failure that missed its goal; what you learned.
"What does a great PM look like to you?"
Graded on whether the candidate can
1. Operate as an equal partner, not a request-taker.
2. Push back on scope/timeline with data, not opinion.
3. Take responsibility for cross-functional outcomes.

Shorter and lower-pressure than the HM round but still gates the loop; verdict often correlates with the HM verdict.

## Worked solution

### Treat the PM or designer as a decision partner

Use actual experience; this is an **illustrative project**. A PM asks for “instant personalized search,” while you own the serving API. Begin by agreeing the user problem—people cannot find the right support article—and a measurable objective, rather than immediately estimating the requested feature list. Ask about the target segment, existing failure examples, deadline and acceptable latency/cost.

Turn ambiguity into options: a lexical improvement this release, hybrid retrieval behind a canary, or a larger personalization project after collecting exposure-aware labels. Explain feasibility with a prototype/trace and the cost of each option. A designer's concern that stale/error messages confuse users is part of product correctness, not a cosmetic request to dismiss. Negotiate a small release with explicit relevance/latency/complaint guardrails and write who owns which decisions.

For disagreement, meet the partner directly, represent their customer/deadline reasoning fairly and compare evidence against the shared goal. If the final decision differs from your preference, commit to it within safe boundaries and monitor the agreed revisit trigger. For success, report the actual user outcome and both teams' contribution. For failure, an example is improved click-through without improved resolution; own the misleading metric choice and revise it rather than blaming product for the goal.

A great PM clarifies whose problem matters, makes trade-offs visible, brings user/business evidence, coordinates decisions and changes course when evidence changes. A great engineering partner contributes feasibility, alternatives and operational consequences while sharing accountability for the outcome. No supplied personal metrics exist, so use real before/after data or clearly state the limited evidence. The story should show a jointly improved decision, not engineering “educating” a nontechnical request-taker.
