## Question

Answer the following conceptual questions on classical machine learning. For each one, give the definition or derivation and a one-line intuition.

### Questions

1. Linear regression: list the assumptions of the model, and explain why squared error is the natural loss.
2. Logistic regression: derive the log loss from the likelihood, and explain why we maximize the log of the likelihood rather than the likelihood itself.
3. Random Forest: how is the feature subset chosen at each split, and why does this reduce the variance of the ensemble?
4. Optimizers: compare SGD with momentum, Adam and AdamW, state the Adam update, and say what AdamW changes.
5. Optimization landscape: given two two-layer networks, one narrow and one wide, which one is more prone to getting trapped in local minima, and why?
Expected answer

A short, correct answer to each part, with derivations where asked and one sentence of intuition per concept.

## Worked solution

### 1. Linear regression

Write $y=X\beta+\epsilon$ with a linear conditional mean and $E[\epsilon\mid X]=0$. Full column rank identifies coefficients; independent errors, constant variance and normality are additional assumptions for conventional efficient estimation/inference, not all prerequisites for computing least squares. Homoskedastic Gaussian errors give negative log likelihood $\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-x_i^T\beta)^2+\text{constant}$, hence squared error. Even without Gaussian errors, squared loss targets $E[Y\mid X]$. **Intuition:** penalizing large deviations strongly fits the average response, but outliers and heteroskedasticity need attention.

### 2. Logistic regression

For $p_i=\sigma(x_i^T\beta)$ and independent binary outcomes, $\mathcal L=\prod_i p_i^{y_i}(1-p_i)^{1-y_i}$. Taking minus the log gives $-\sum_i[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)]$; its mean is log loss. Log is strictly increasing, so maximizing it preserves the likelihood optimum, turns products into sums and avoids underflow. Use a stable logits implementation. **Intuition:** assign high probability to what actually happened rather than merely choosing the right side of .5.

### 3. Random forest

For each split independently sample a subset of candidate features (often size approximately $\sqrt p$ in classification, but a tuned parameter), then choose the best split among them. Bootstrap rows and feature randomness diversify trees. With equal tree variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise error correlation $\rho$, variance of a T-tree average is $\sigma^2[\rho+(1-\rho)/T]$. Feature subsampling lowers correlation, though too few candidates can increase individual-tree bias. **Intuition:** averaging helps most when trees make different mistakes.

### 4. Optimizers

Momentum SGD uses $v_t=\beta v_{t-1}+g_t$ and $\theta_t=\theta_{t-1}-\eta v_t$ under this convention. Adam uses

$$m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t,\quad v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2,$$
$$\hat m_t=m_t/(1-\beta_1^t),\quad\hat v_t=v_t/(1-\beta_2^t),\quad\theta_t=\theta_{t-1}-\eta\hat m_t/(\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon).$$

Adam adapts per-coordinate scale and is often convenient for sparse/noisy gradients; momentum SGD is simple, uses less optimizer state and can generalize well after tuning. AdamW replaces coupled L2-gradient regularization with decoupled shrinkage: $\theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)\theta_{t-1}-\eta\hat m_t/(\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon)$, moments formed from the data gradient. **Intuition:** momentum smooths directions, Adam rescales them, and AdamW makes weight shrinkage independent of that rescaling.

### 5. Width and local minima

There is no universal answer based on width alone: activation, data, loss, initialization and optimization matter. Under useful overparameterized settings a wider two-layer network has more ways to interpolate and often easier optimization; narrow networks can have restrictive bottlenecks and poor minima. Specific global-convergence results require specific assumptions and do not cover arbitrary networks. Saddles, plateaus and poor conditioning can be more important than strict bad minima. **Intuition:** extra hidden units create more possible routes to a fit, but do not certify that a chosen optimizer reaches it.
