<pre class="question-text" style="white-space:pre-wrap;font-family:inherit;line-height:1.55">QR Technical Round — Brainteasers &amp; Estimation

A traditional QR technical round mixing probability/statistics brainteasers with Fermi estimation: invariant-style stone games, expected waiting-time questions, and market-sizing with confidence intervals.

Requirements
A verbal technical round (one of ~6 QR rounds) testing probability, statistics, and quantitative reasoning through brainteasers and estimation.
Examples

Practice questions:

Two piles of stones, one with 1 and one with 24. Each turn, take from the larger pile an amount equal to the smaller pile (doubling the smaller): (1, 24) → (2, 23) → … Can repeated moves ever empty one pile? What about starting (1, 25) or (1, 23)? Prove under which initial conditions a pile can be emptied.
A bus comes exactly every 10 minutes; you arrive at a random time. Expected wait for the next bus? And if the bus is late with 50% probability, how does the expected wait change?
Estimate how many houses the total annual U.S. spend on Netflix could buy; give a 90% confidence interval. If told there are 81M U.S. subscribers, how does your interval change?
Notes
The stone-game question is an invariant/number-theory proof (think in terms of parity / the binary representation of the pile sizes), not a calculation.
The estimation questions want a structured Fermi breakdown plus an honest confidence interval that tightens when you&#x27;re given a concrete data point.</pre>

## Worked solution

1. For piles (a,b), total S=a+b is invariant. A transfer doubles the smaller pile, and modulo S the relevant residue doubles up to sign/swapping. A pile reaches zero iff S/gcd(a,b) is a power of two: the odd part must already divide the starting pile.
2. Thus (1,24), (1,25) and (1,23) never empty a pile: their reduced totals 25,26 and 24 are not powers of two. A contrasting (1,7) reaches (2,6),(4,4),(8,0).
3. For deterministic ten-minute bus intervals and uniform arrival phase, expected wait is five minutes. A 50% late probability alone is insufficient; in a stationary renewal model E[wait]=E[X²]/(2E[X]) if the required moments exist, so the amount/dependence of lateness matters.
4. For the spending estimate, houses=(subscribers*12*monthly_price)/house_price. Propagate explicitly assumed uncertainty ranges and update the subscriber component when 81M is given; no current price or house-cost data is supplied.

### Small example / sanity check

Assuming 81M subscribers, 15 currency units/month and 400000/unit house cost gives 36450 houses/year. These are declared arithmetic assumptions, not measured market facts.

### Common trap

Using parity alone as a complete invariant proof, or giving a unique delayed-bus wait without a lateness distribution.
