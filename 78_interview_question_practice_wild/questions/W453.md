## Question

QR Technical Round — Brainteasers & Estimation

A traditional QR technical round mixing probability/statistics brainteasers with Fermi estimation: invariant-style stone games, expected waiting-time questions, and market-sizing with confidence intervals.

### Requirements

A verbal technical round (one of ~6 QR rounds) testing probability, statistics, and quantitative reasoning through brainteasers and estimation.

### Examples

Practice questions:

Two piles of stones, one with 1 and one with 24. Each turn, take from the larger pile an amount equal to the smaller pile (doubling the smaller): (1, 24) → (2, 23) → … Can repeated moves ever empty one pile? What about starting (1, 25) or (1, 23)? Prove under which initial conditions a pile can be emptied.
A bus comes exactly every 10 minutes; you arrive at a random time. Expected wait for the next bus? And if the bus is late with 50% probability, how does the expected wait change?
Estimate how many houses the total annual U.S. spend on Netflix could buy; give a 90% confidence interval. If told there are 81M U.S. subscribers, how does your interval change?
Notes
The stone-game question is an invariant/number-theory proof (think in terms of parity / the binary representation of the pile sizes), not a calculation.
The estimation questions want a structured Fermi breakdown plus an honest confidence interval that tightens when you're given a concrete data point.

## Worked solution

### Stone transfer is modular doubling

Interpret the example as transferring the smaller pile into itself: from labeled sizes a, b with total S, the first pile becomes $a'\equiv2a\pmod S$ (if it is larger, subtract b; if smaller, double it). One pile empties iff $S\mid2^ta$ for some t, equivalently $S/\gcd(a,b)$ is a power of two. Odd factors cannot be removed by repeated doubling; conversely a power-of-two quotient eventually divides 2^t. Therefore (1, 24), (1, 25) and (1, 23) **all cannot** empty, since totals 25, 26, 24 have odd factors after reduction. (1, 31) can: its total 32 is a power of two. This follows the supplied transfer example; a different stone-removal game would have different invariants.

### Waiting needs the arrival/lateness model

For a perfectly periodic 10-minute bus and an independent uniform arrival phase, wait is uniform [0, 10), mean 5 minutes. “Late with probability 50%” does not specify how late or its effect on headways, so no unique second answer exists. If all headways are iid 5 or 15 minutes equally often (mean 10), a random-time arrival sees the inspection paradox: $E[W]=E[H^2]/(2E[H])=125/20=6.25$ minutes. If instead you arrive uniformly in a scheduled 10-minute interval and that particular bus is independently delayed exactly d minutes without another intervening bus, expected wait is 5+d/2. State which model applies.

### A Fermi interval is an assumption-driven range

For a labeled planning estimate, assume 80M paying US accounts, USD 15/month realized spend and USD 400K per house: annual spend ≈ USD 14.4B, equivalent to 36,000 houses. A plausible subjective 90% range might start with accounts 60–100M, monthly spend USD 10–20 and house price USD 300–600K; multiplying all extremes yields 12,000–80,000, a sensitivity envelope, **not** a calibrated 90% interval. To produce a 90% interval, assign a joint distribution to these uncertain inputs and simulate its 5th/95th percentiles, showing correlation assumptions. Given 81M subscribers, fix that factor and narrow only its uncertainty; price/house uncertainty remains, and “subscribers” must match accounts rather than individual viewers. These are hypothetical inputs, not current Netflix revenue or housing statistics.
