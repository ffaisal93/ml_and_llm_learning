## Question

DS Power Day case round. The interviewer presents a concrete credit/lending business scenario and asks you to frame the analysis, decompose the P&L, and propose levers. Mental math is part of the round. Examples reported:

Should we offer line-of-credit increases to a specific customer segment? Forecast revenue and default impact.
A new credit product is launching in a new geography — analyse expected profitability.
A risk-model change has been proposed — what is the P&L sensitivity?

Walk through: (1) clarify the goal (revenue uplift vs default reduction vs combined profit), (2) decompose into a P&L equation, (3) identify levers, (4) do an approximate mental-math sensitivity check on the dominant lever. Do not jump to modelling — the graded signal is business decomposition first.

## Worked solution

### Frame an incremental lending decision

For a credit-line increase, compare expected risk-adjusted profit under the new policy with profit under the no-change policy. The actual cohort and financial inputs are not supplied, so the calculation below is an explicitly hypothetical example. Clarify the horizon, eligible customers, expected usage, interest/fee recognition, default definition, recoveries, funding, servicing and any capital requirement before choosing a model.

A one-period approximation for a roughly homogeneous cohort is

$$
\Delta\Pi=N\left[\Delta B(y-c_f)-\Delta(PD\cdot EAD\cdot LGD)
+\Delta\mathrm{fees}-\Delta\mathrm{servicing}\right]
-\Delta\mathrm{fixed\ costs}-\Delta\mathrm{capital\ charge}.
$$

Here N is customer count, $\Delta B$ is incremental average drawn balance per customer, y is interest yield before credit losses, and $c_f$ is funding rate. PD is default probability over the same horizon, EAD is exposure at default and LGD is the loss fraction after recoveries. The bracketed amounts are per-customer currency; fixed costs and capital charge are cohort totals. The change in expected loss is the new-policy loss minus the old-policy loss, not automatically a change in PD alone. Use a discounted monthly cash-flow model when timing, repayments, delinquency or recoveries materially affect value.

### Work a mental-math example

Assume 10,000 customers receive 1,000 USD of additional limit and draw an average of 20%, giving 200 USD incremental balance each. Let annual interest yield be 12%, funding cost 4%, extra servicing 4 USD/customer, and additional fixed/capital costs zero for this illustration.

| Component | Per customer | Cohort total |
|---|---:|---:|
| Net interest spread: $200(0.12-0.04)$ | 16 USD | 160,000 USD |
| Additional servicing | −4 USD | −40,000 USD |
| Amount available before credit losses | 12 USD | 120,000 USD |
| Assumed incremental expected loss | −8 USD | −80,000 USD |
| Incremental annual profit | 4 USD | 40,000 USD |

Break-even incremental loss is therefore 12 USD/customer. For sensitivity, additionally assume incremental EAD is 200 USD and LGD is 50%. A one-percentage-point PD increase adds

$$200\times0.5\times0.01=1\ \text{USD/customer},$$

or 10,000 USD across the cohort. Four percentage points would erase the illustrated 40,000 USD profit. A credit limit is not EAD, however, and a policy-induced PD change can affect existing balances too. Recalculate the complete affected book when that is true. Keep interest recognition consistent with expected-loss accounting so the same default loss is not charged twice.

### Identify coupled levers before modeling

Revenue levers include take-up, utilization, pricing, fees and retention. Risk levers include eligibility, limit size, PD/EAD/LGD calibration and collections. Costs include funding, servicing, acquisition, fraud, launch work and capital. These interact: increasing price can reduce take-up or change borrower selection, so do not optimize each spreadsheet cell independently.

For a new geography, add local demand, distribution, payment operations, currency exposure and launch costs; determine the relevant local requirements rather than inventing current rules. For a proposed model change, translate score/threshold changes into actual approvals, balances and losses. A 1% AUC gain does not imply a 1% profit gain.

### Decide what evidence would change the recommendation

Use mature, time-valid cohorts and compare against the no-change policy. Check segment calibration, selection effects and uncertainty in the dominant driver. A controlled eligible-cohort rollout or credible causal design is needed before attributing retention/default changes to an offer. Set loss, customer-outcome and operational guardrails, and specify a rollback threshold. Present a sensitivity range and the missing evidence that would reverse the decision rather than an unsupported precise forecast.
