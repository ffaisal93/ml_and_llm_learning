## Question

Applied Scientist round built around an under-defined business problem. The interviewer states a vague, stripped-down prompt and waits; you must clarify goals, propose a plan, and reason through tradeoffs aloud.

From the official module: "A critical skill for this role is dealing with ambiguous and ill-defined problems... This module tests your ability to productively discuss a problem and walk through what you would do and why. The stated problem will be slightly understated / vague / undefined in the beginning."

Topic areas seen: search/ranking/experimentation, brand-safety scoring, generation-quality assessment.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

#### Structure an ML system-design answer.

Start from the decision the system must support, then connect data, modeling, serving and evaluation.

1. Clarify users, target, prediction time, latency, scale and error costs; state assumptions if details are missing.
2. Design data collection and labels, a baseline, offline evaluation, serving interfaces and feedback loops.
3. Estimate capacity and discuss failures, monitoring, rollback and abuse. Spend deep-dive time on the hardest constraint rather than listing every database technology.

#### Prepare a research experiment with limited compute.

Spend the budget on experiments that most clearly distinguish hypotheses.

1. State the hypothesis, expected observable effect and a simple falsifying result.
2. Choose a small faithful setting, strong baseline and controlled ablation before scaling.
3. Track compute, seeds and failures, and reserve budget for confirmation rather than spending everything on a broad unreplicated search.

#### Separate correlation, prediction and causation.

A useful predictive association need not describe the effect of intervening on a feature.

1. State whether the goal is forecasting, explanation or estimating an intervention effect.
2. List plausible confounders and timing relationships; check selection and measurement mechanisms.
3. Use randomization or a defensible causal identification strategy for causal claims, with explicit assumptions and sensitivity checks.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Give evidence for a decision, not a polished but untestable story

Use an actual experience and separate your responsibility from the team's. State the problem and constraint, the alternative you considered, your action and the observable result. If you do not have a defensible metric, say what evidence you do have instead of inventing a percentage.

For a technical project, explain the data/split, baseline, hardest failure and the experiment that changed your mind. For conflict or feedback, represent the other person's concern fairly and show how you tested assumptions or changed behavior. “I won the argument” is not the project outcome.

For a research claim, distinguish mechanism from evidence. A bigger training dataset or compute budget can confound an architecture comparison. Ask which ablation separates the proposed explanation from a plausible alternative and whether uncertainty is large relative to the claimed gain.

A useful spoken structure is problem → constraint → decision → evidence → limitation → next step. Keep a longer derivation or failure trace ready for follow-up rather than hiding weak reasoning behind jargon. A motivation/timeline question needs a direct truthful answer, not a generic success story; protect confidential details and never fabricate offers or deadlines.

### Work a small example

For spam detection, the correct latency and false-positive cost differ between pre-publication blocking and later review.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Drawing infrastructure before defining what counts as a correct model decision.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**Prepare a research experiment with limited compute.** Testing a new loss on a small task with known failure modes can be more informative than one expensive full-model run with many changes.

What if a small-scale result fails to transfer? Identify which scaling assumptions changed and test them explicitly.

**Separate correlation, prediction and causation.** Umbrella use predicts rain but making people carry umbrellas does not cause rain.

Can a noncausal predictor still be useful? Yes for prediction, subject to availability, stability and policy constraints.

### Explain it back

Close the answer. State the main invariant or equation, work the example with changed inputs, and explain which assumption would make your solution fail. For a design, walk through a retry or partial failure; for a derivative, check a shape and a finite-difference case.
