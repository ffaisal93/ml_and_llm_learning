## Question

Eight short probability and statistics problems, each with a single numeric or closed-form answer. They are meant to be solved quickly and cleanly by hand: recognise the standard distribution or counting argument, then compute.

Answer each of the following. Give exact values (fractions or closed forms) where possible; for item 2 also give a decimal rounded to three places.
1. Constrained maximum. Let P = (px, py) be the point in the first quadrant (px &gt;= 0, py &gt;= 0) on the unit circle x^2 + y^2 = 1 that maximizes f(x, y) = x^2 * y. Find py.
2. Continuous density. A random variable X has density f(x) = 0.5 * e^(-|x|) for all real x. Find P(2 &lt;= |X| &lt;= 4).
3. Conditional cards. A 5-card hand is dealt from a standard 52-card deck and is known to contain at least 3 aces. Find the probability that it contains all 4 aces.
4. Dice sum. A fair six-sided die is rolled 5 times. Find the probability that the sum of the five rolls is a multiple of 5.
5. Normalising constant. A discrete random variable takes values 1, 2, ..., 12 with P(X = x) = k * (2x - 1). Find k.
6. Coin runs. A fair coin is tossed 9 times. A run is a maximal block of consecutive tosses with the same outcome. Find the expected number of runs.
7. Standard error. X1, ..., X100 are independent standard normal variables and Y is their average. Find the standard deviation of Y.
8. Dice maximum. Three fair six-sided dice are rolled and the score is the largest face. Find P(score = 2).

### Requirements

Show the key step for each item (the formula or counting argument), not only the final number.
State assumptions you use, such as independence of rolls and tosses.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

1. 1: substitute x²=1-y², maximizing y-y³ on [0,1]; derivative 1-3y² gives y=1/sqrt(3). 2: the two Laplace tails give exp(-2)-exp(-4)≈0.117020, rounded to 0.117.
2. 3: P(4 aces|at least 3)=C(48,1)/[C(4,3)C(48,2)+C(48,1)]=1/95. 4: convolve five die residues modulo 5; favorable count is (6^5+4)/5=1556, so probability 389/1944≈0.200103.
3. 5: sum_(x=1)^12(2x-1)=12²=144, so k=1/144. 6: runs=1+sum_(i=2)^9 I(change), expectation 1+8/2=5.
4. 7: independent standard normals averaged over 100 give variance 1/100 and standard deviation 0.1. 8: P(max=2)=P(all<=2)-P(all<=1)=(2/6)^3-(1/6)^3=7/216.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Name the random experiment and condition on the right event

Write the sample space and independence assumptions before counting. Bayes' rule reverses conditioning:

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0.
$$

A $90\%$ detection rate is not a $90\%$ probability that an alert is genuine. Prevalence and false-positive rate determine the latter. Work expected counts in a hypothetical population when the formula feels abstract.

For waiting-time and random-walk exercises, first-step analysis often gives a solvable recurrence:

$$
h(s)=1+\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s)h(s'),\qquad h(s)=0\text{ at absorbing states}.
$$

Specify what counts as one step: two particles moving once each may cost two coin flips but only one elapsed second. At a forced horizon, all surviving probability mass must be assigned to the final stopping time.

For expected counts, indicator variables and linearity do not require independent indicators. For uncertainty or variance, dependence does matter. Check normalization, limiting cases and a small enumeration. Simulation is useful validation but not a replacement for a requested exact derivation; conversely, a neat-looking fraction should not be asserted merely because a numerical estimate is close to it.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Confusing standard deviation with variance or treating the five dice-sum residues as exactly uniform.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**Use Bayes' rule for a rare-event detector.** With prevalence 1%, sensitivity 90% and false-positive rate 5%, precision is 0.009/(0.009+0.0495), about 15.4%.

What if prevalence rises? Precision rises under unchanged sensitivity and false-positive rate, though real conditional rates may also change.

**Compute the expected minimum of uniform samples.** For three independent draws, the expected minimum is 1/4, even though each draw's mean is 1/2.

What is the expected maximum? n/(n+1), by symmetry or its distribution function t^n.

**Design a Monte Carlo estimate and quantify its error.** Reducing Monte Carlo standard error by a factor of two generally requires four times as many independent samples.

What if the distribution has infinite variance? Ordinary square-root-N error reasoning may fail.
