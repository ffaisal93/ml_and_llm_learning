## Question

A discussion question on equity quantitative research: validating alpha factors, then reasoning about the noise of an LLM-based signal. Answer all six parts.

### Questions

1. How do you detect whether a factor uses future (look-ahead) data?
2. How do you set Information Coefficient (IC) and IC information ratio (ICIR) thresholds for factor selection?
3. How do you train a factor model, and what is the training objective?
4. How do you prevent overfitting when many candidate factors are tested?
5. Walk through an end-to-end alpha-research workflow, from hypothesis to live monitoring.
6. Case study, LLM inference stability. An LLM labels each day's news with a sentiment class using temperature &gt; 0, so its output is non-deterministic. Day 1 gives labels y1 and day 2 gives labels y2 for the same items, with corr(y1, y2) = 0.95. Two independent aggregates are each built by averaging the labels of N independent days. State your noise model, then find the smallest integer N for which the correlation between the two N-day aggregates is at least 0.99.
Deliverable

For each part, give a concrete, defensible answer. For part 6, derive N from stated assumptions and show the arithmetic.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

1. Audit factor timestamps and availability, corporate-action handling, survivorship and future-normalization leakage. Define a forecast horizon, baseline, costs/turnover and time-based validation with appropriate overlap controls.
2. IC is a chosen cross-sectional forecast/outcome correlation, often rank correlation. ICIR requires an explicit time aggregation/annualization convention; there is no universal good threshold independent of sample dependence, breadth and costs.
3. Fit a stated prediction/portfolio objective, use nested or held-out selection for many factors, and validate after costs on untouched periods. Track drift, capacity and realized-vs-predicted behavior after deployment.
4. For stability, one possible model is repeated measurements y=S+epsilon with a common latent signal and independent equal-variance errors. rho_1=0.95 implies noise/signal variance=1/19; averaging N repetitions gives rho_N=1/(1+1/(19N)), so N>=ceil(99/19)=6 reaches 0.99.
5. If instead both aggregates average N independent changing daily signals with matching independent noise, signal and noise variances both shrink by N and correlation stays 0.95. The wording alone does not justify a fixed-signal averaging model; state it explicitly.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Name the random experiment and condition on the right event

Write the sample space and independence assumptions before counting. Bayes' rule reverses conditioning:

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0.
$$

A $90\%$ detection rate is not a $90\%$ probability that an alert is genuine. Prevalence and false-positive rate determine the latter. Work expected counts in a hypothetical population when the formula feels abstract.

For waiting-time and random-walk exercises, first-step analysis often gives a solvable recurrence:

$$
h(s)=1+\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s)h(s'),\qquad h(s)=0\text{ at absorbing states}.
$$

Specify what counts as one step: two particles moving once each may cost two coin flips but only one elapsed second. At a forced horizon, all surviving probability mass must be assigned to the final stopping time.

For expected counts, indicator variables and linearity do not require independent indicators. For uncertainty or variance, dependence does matter. Check normalization, limiting cases and a small enumeration. Simulation is useful validation but not a replacement for a requested exact derivation; conversely, a neat-looking fraction should not be asserted merely because a numerical estimate is close to it.

#### Put the result in mathematical notation

For repeated observations of one fixed latent signal with independent equal-variance noise,
$$\rho_N=\frac1{1+(\rho_1^{-1}-1)/N}.$$
With $\rho_1=0.95$, requiring $\rho_N\ge0.99$ gives $N\ge99/19$ and thus $N=6$. If both latent signals and errors are independently averaged across changing days, both variances shrink at the same rate and the correlation need not improve. State the model before the number.

### Work a small example

Under the repeated-fixed-signal model, N=5 gives 95/96≈0.98958 and N=6 gives 114/115≈0.99130. Under independent daily-signal averaging, increasing N does not improve the ratio.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Quoting N=6 without identifying the noise/signal assumptions, or selecting factors by one favorable in-sample IC.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**How should you split data for reliable model selection?** A patient's visits belong in the same fold if the deployment target is unseen patients; otherwise identity patterns can leak across folds.

When is a random split reasonable? When examples are sufficiently independent and identically distributed relative to the intended use.

**Estimate a confidence interval for a paired model comparison.** If the same difficult examples fool both models, treating the two accuracy estimates as independent discards useful covariance information.

What if the final metric is nonlinear? Recompute that metric on each paired bootstrap sample rather than averaging inappropriate per-example surrogates.

**Separate correlation, prediction and causation.** Umbrella use predicts rain but making people carry umbrellas does not cause rain.

Can a noncausal predictor still be useful? Yes for prediction, subject to availability, stability and policy constraints.
