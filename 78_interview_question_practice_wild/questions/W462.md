## Question

<pre class="question-text" style="white-space:pre-wrap;font-family:inherit;line-height:1.55">Final HM round, typically 30-45 min, mixing standard STAR-format behavioral questions with one extra project deep-dive — and on the director / M2 variant, motivation and impact rather than implementation details.

Requirements

Final-round HM call. Two reported flavors:

HM (direct manager):

Project deep-dive on something different from earlier rounds (do not repeat).
Standard STAR behavioral set: most challenging project, conflict with a teammate, handling ambiguity, a time you failed, how you prioritize tasks.
Light &quot;fit&quot; probing: why this team, why ByteDance / TikTok, longer-term career direction.
Product-scope challenge: final leaders may question whether your project was product-effective, whether the scope was right, and whether the impact justifies the technical work.

Director / M2 variant:

Shorter (~20 minutes).
Pick a recent piece of work distinct from earlier deep-dives and walk through it.
Director-level questions focus on motivation and impact, not implementation. Expect &quot;why did this matter?&quot;, &quot;what was the business outcome?&quot;, &quot;what would you have done with more time / more headcount?&quot;.
Notes
The &quot;tell me something new&quot; rule is strict. Do not repeat the project you used in earlier rounds, or interviewers will suspect a narrow portfolio.
Director rounds reward outcome framing over technical depth — answer in terms of business / user impact, then drop into a one-sentence technical summary only if pressed.
Bilingual flexibility expected at HM rounds; very common for HM to switch to Chinese mid-conversation if the candidate is comfortable.
Recurring pitfall: candidates who STAR-format every answer mechanically and lose the conversational thread. Treat HM as a peer conversation, not a structured-interview drill.
Salary expectations are usually surfaced separately by the recruiter post-HM, not during the HM call itself — though some HMs ask about timeline and competing offers.</pre>

## Worked solution

1. How do you handle disagreement over an ML design? Make the disputed assumptions testable and connect the decision to the shared objective.
2. Restate the other person's concern fairly and identify whether the disagreement concerns facts, constraints or preferences.
3. Propose a small experiment or decision table with agreed success criteria, cost and deadline.
4. Document the decision, support execution after it is made and revisit only when meaningful new evidence appears.
5. Explain a project failure constructively. Show ownership, evidence and changed behavior, not a story in which someone else is always at fault.
6. State the goal, your responsibility and what failed in observable terms.
7. Explain which assumption or decision was wrong and how you detected it.
8. Describe recovery, what changed afterward and whether the fix actually helped. Keep confidential details appropriately abstract.
9. Handle harsh technical feedback. Separate the useful content of feedback from its delivery and verify what needs to change.
10. Listen, summarize the specific concern and ask for concrete examples when necessary.
11. Check the evidence, acknowledge valid mistakes and propose a bounded correction with a follow-up date.
12. If disagreement remains, explain it respectfully using the shared goal and evidence, and seek the appropriate resolution channel.

### Apply the exact contract

Prepare separate real examples for conflict, failure, leadership, inclusion and feedback. Use situation→your responsibility→specific actions→measured result→lesson. A video round needs concise complete responses; a values round needs evidence of behavior, not a memorized list of slogans. When a prompt asks several questions, do not reuse one anecdote without answering the different decisions it probes.

### Small example / sanity check

A debate over retrieval complexity can become a measured comparison of answer quality, p95 latency and maintenance cost on a fixed query set.

### Common trap

Describing a colleague as incompetent or presenting “I won the argument” as the result.
