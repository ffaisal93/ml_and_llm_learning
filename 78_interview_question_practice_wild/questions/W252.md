## Question

Explain the CAP theorem and use it to reason about a replicated service during a network partition. The response should distinguish guarantees from common database labels and turn the theorem into concrete design decisions.

### Questions

1. Define consistency, availability, and partition tolerance precisely in the CAP model.
2. Explain why a system experiencing a partition cannot guarantee both consistency and availability for every request.
3. For two replicas separated by a partition, trace what a consistency-first design does and what an availability-first design does when reads and writes arrive on both sides.
4. Give representative consistency-first and availability-first systems, qualifying any configurable behavior rather than assigning a permanent label.
5. Explain why describing CAP as simply "choose any two" is misleading, and relate CAP to the latency-versus-consistency choice captured by PACELC when no partition exists.
6. Recommend policies for the two scenarios below, including client-visible errors, conflict handling, and recovery after communication resumes.
Scenarios
A replicated payment ledger must prevent conflicting committed balances, even if one partition cannot serve writes temporarily.
A reaction counter should accept updates during a regional isolation and may converge after replicas reconnect.

### Constraints

Assume asynchronous communication: messages can be delayed or lost long enough that a node cannot distinguish a partition from a slow peer.
Use linearizability for CAP consistency and a response from every non-failing node for CAP availability.
Discuss behavior during the partition and reconciliation afterward; normal-operation replication alone is not enough.

## Worked solution

### Guarantees and the impossibility trace

CAP consistency means **linearizability**: completed operations appear in one real-time-respecting order on a single logical object. Availability means every request received by a non-failing node eventually completes with an appropriate operation response; a timeout/error instead of a required read/write result sacrifices that guarantee. Partition tolerance means the system must operate under arbitrarily delayed/lost inter-node messages; it is an assumed failure condition, not an optional feature one can disable in an asynchronous network.

Replicas A and B initially store x=0, then cannot communicate. A accepts and acknowledges write x=1. A subsequent read at B cannot distinguish this world from one where no write happened. Returning 0 violates linearizability in the first world. Waiting indefinitely for A violates availability under permanent partition; rejecting the read also gives up CAP availability. Accepting independent conflicting writes on both sides makes the same tension visible. A failure timeout cannot tell a slow peer from an isolated one.

### Two practical policies

A consistency-first replicated ledger uses majority consensus and a fenced leader. With only two voting replicas, a partition leaves no majority on either side; add a third voter/failure domain to keep one majority side progressing. The minority rejects authoritative writes/linearizable reads with retryable unavailable/not-leader errors. A timeout after submission is UNKNOWN to the client, so it queries/retries with the same transaction id. After repair, committed log entries win and uncommitted conflicting proposals are discarded; never merge balances with last-write-wins or replay a transfer twice. Read-only stale status can be offered separately with a clearly weaker contract, but not described as a current committed balance.

An availability-first reaction counter accepts local uniquely identified reactions on both sides and converges later. For increment-only counting use a per-replica grow-only counter and component-wise max merge; for like/unlike semantics use an observed-remove set or per-user versioned reaction state, since blindly adding retries/unlikes is incorrect. Return the local approximate count with freshness metadata. Merge commutatively/idempotently on repair, reconcile duplicates and inspect dropped events; there is no global instantaneous linearizable count during isolation.

### Labels and the non-partition trade-off

Representative consistency-first behavior includes consensus-backed coordination/log systems serving quorum operations. Dynamo-style systems can favor available local writes with version/conflict reconciliation. Configuration matters: quorum levels, leader reads, stale follower reads, multi-region modes and operation type change guarantees, so a database brand is not a permanent CAP label. A locally returned error is not a clever way to provide both model guarantees.

“Choose any two” hides that real networks partition and the trade-off applies to particular operations during that condition. Outside partitions, synchronous remote coordination also adds latency; PACELC summarizes “if Partition, Availability versus Consistency; Else, Latency versus Consistency.” It is a design lens, not another proof or a complete failure taxonomy. The ledger chooses stronger coordination and temporary unavailability because conflicting money commits are unacceptable. The reaction product chooses convergence and duplicate-safe updates because temporarily stale counts are acceptable. Exercise writes/reads on both sides and recovery in tests; normal replication on a healthy network does not establish either partition policy.
