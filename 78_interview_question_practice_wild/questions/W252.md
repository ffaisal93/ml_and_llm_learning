## Question

Explain the CAP theorem and use it to reason about a replicated service during a network partition. The response should distinguish guarantees from common database labels and turn the theorem into concrete design decisions.

### Questions

1. Define consistency, availability, and partition tolerance precisely in the CAP model.
2. Explain why a system experiencing a partition cannot guarantee both consistency and availability for every request.
3. For two replicas separated by a partition, trace what a consistency-first design does and what an availability-first design does when reads and writes arrive on both sides.
4. Give representative consistency-first and availability-first systems, qualifying any configurable behavior rather than assigning a permanent label.
5. Explain why describing CAP as simply "choose any two" is misleading, and relate CAP to the latency-versus-consistency choice captured by PACELC when no partition exists.
6. Recommend policies for the two scenarios below, including client-visible errors, conflict handling, and recovery after communication resumes.
Scenarios
A replicated payment ledger must prevent conflicting committed balances, even if one partition cannot serve writes temporarily.
A reaction counter should accept updates during a regional isolation and may converge after replicas reconnect.

### Constraints

Assume asynchronous communication: messages can be delayed or lost long enough that a node cannot distinguish a partition from a slow peer.
Use linearizability for CAP consistency and a response from every non-failing node for CAP availability.
Discuss behavior during the partition and reconciliation afterward; normal-operation replication alone is not enough.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

Under the CAP model, a partitioned service cannot guarantee both linearizable behavior and a successful available response to every request at every non-failing node.

1. State what counts as consistency and availability in this model.
2. Trace conflicting reads and writes on isolated replicas: a consistency-first design may reject or delay some operations; an availability-first design may accept divergence.
3. Explain reconciliation after communication returns and normal-operation latency tradeoffs separately.

### Understand why the approach works

#### State what an acknowledgment guarantees

Distinguish accepted in memory, appended to a local file, flushed durably, replicated and committed. A client-visible success must correspond to the promised boundary; otherwise recovery can lose operations the client believes are complete.

A retry is not necessarily a new logical operation. Use a stable operation id, bind it to request parameters, and atomically record its state and result with the business change where possible. If a worker finishes an effect and crashes before acknowledgment, the repeated id lets a recipient recognize it.

Timeouts mean lack of observed response, not proof that no action happened. Leases and heartbeats detect suspected failure; fencing tokens prevent an expired owner from committing after a successor took over. In a partition, stronger consistency may require rejecting some requests rather than allowing conflicting leaders.

Walk through one failure between each pair of durable transitions. For tree aggregation, record one contribution per child and request id; duplicate messages must not increase the count. For an external effect, reconcile uncertain outcomes rather than silently retrying under a new identity. Explain liveness assumptions separately: permanent loss of access to required state can make guaranteed completion impossible even when the protocol preserves safety.

### Work a small example

A ledger and a reaction counter can reasonably choose different policies because the consequences of conflicts differ.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Reducing CAP to “choose any two” without discussing an actual partition or the meaning of guarantees.
