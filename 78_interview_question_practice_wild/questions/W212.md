## Question

Walk the interviewer through the most technically complex project you have led or made a major contribution to. This is a project deep dive: the interviewer wants to see how you think about trade-offs, scope and impact, and how you lead other engineers, not only what was built. Plan for about 10–15 minutes of story, then follow-up questions on the parts below.

What to answer
1. Context and your role: what problem the project solved, why it mattered to the business or users, and what you personally owned (as opposed to the team).
2. Key technical trade-offs: two or three decisions you made, the options you rejected, and why. In hindsight, what would you do differently?
3. Scoping: how you decided what went into the first version and what was cut or deferred.
4. Measuring success: the metrics you defined at the start, and the measured outcome (numbers, with the baseline you compare against).
5. Driving the decision across the team: one significant architectural decision you pushed through, who disagreed, and how you built agreement.
What a strong answer shows
Concrete, measurable impact, not just involvement.
Ownership: clear about what "I" did versus what "we" did.
Principled decisions under ambiguity, with the reasoning stated.
Honesty about mistakes and what changed afterwards.
Enough technical depth that every claim survives one more "why?".

### Constraints

One project, told end to end; do not switch projects between parts.
Answer each part in 1–3 minutes when asked as a follow-up.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

#### Explain your most technically complex ML project.

A strong answer demonstrates decisions and evidence, not a list of technologies.

1. State the user problem, constraints and your own contribution separately from the team's work.
2. Describe the data, baseline, hardest technical failure, alternatives considered and why the chosen approach fit.
3. Quantify the result using a valid comparison, explain limitations and say what you would change next. Use real experiences rather than invented metrics.

#### Discuss the data strategy behind an ML project.

Data choices can explain more of a project's outcome than the architecture name.

1. Describe collection, permissions, label generation, cleaning and the intended deployment distribution.
2. Explain split design, duplicates, hard examples, reviewer quality and versioning.
3. Connect one data intervention to a controlled result and identify unresolved gaps.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Give evidence for a decision, not a polished but untestable story

Use an actual experience and separate your responsibility from the team's. State the problem and constraint, the alternative you considered, your action and the observable result. If you do not have a defensible metric, say what evidence you do have instead of inventing a percentage.

For a technical project, explain the data/split, baseline, hardest failure and the experiment that changed your mind. For conflict or feedback, represent the other person's concern fairly and show how you tested assumptions or changed behavior. “I won the argument” is not the project outcome.

For a research claim, distinguish mechanism from evidence. A bigger training dataset or compute budget can confound an architecture comparison. Ask which ablation separates the proposed explanation from a plausible alternative and whether uncertainty is large relative to the claimed gain.

A useful spoken structure is problem → constraint → decision → evidence → limitation → next step. Keep a longer derivation or failure trace ready for follow-up rather than hiding weak reasoning behind jargon. A motivation/timeline question needs a direct truthful answer, not a generic success story; protect confidential details and never fabricate offers or deadlines.

### Work a small example

“I reduced p95 latency from 800 to 500 ms on a matched workload while keeping task accuracy within the predefined margin” states both benefit and guardrail.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Saying “we built an LLM system” without explaining your choices or how success was measured.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**Discuss the data strategy behind an ML project.** Improving annotation guidelines while holding the model fixed can show a clearer causal contribution than changing data and architecture together.

What would you collect next? Prioritize examples that address measured failure modes and important missing slices.

### Explain it back

Close the answer. State the main invariant or equation, work the example with changed inputs, and explain which assumption would make your solution fail. For a design, walk through a retry or partial failure; for a derivative, check a shape and a finite-difference case.
