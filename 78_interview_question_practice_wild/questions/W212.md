## Question

Walk the interviewer through the most technically complex project you have led or made a major contribution to. This is a project deep dive: the interviewer wants to see how you think about trade-offs, scope and impact, and how you lead other engineers, not only what was built. Plan for about 10–15 minutes of story, then follow-up questions on the parts below.

What to answer
1. Context and your role: what problem the project solved, why it mattered to the business or users, and what you personally owned (as opposed to the team).
2. Key technical trade-offs: two or three decisions you made, the options you rejected, and why. In hindsight, what would you do differently?
3. Scoping: how you decided what went into the first version and what was cut or deferred.
4. Measuring success: the metrics you defined at the start, and the measured outcome (numbers, with the baseline you compare against).
5. Driving the decision across the team: one significant architectural decision you pushed through, who disagreed, and how you built agreement.
What a strong answer shows
Concrete, measurable impact, not just involvement.
Ownership: clear about what "I" did versus what "we" did.
Principled decisions under ambiguity, with the reasoning stated.
Honesty about mistakes and what changed afterwards.
Enough technical depth that every claim survives one more "why?".

### Constraints

One project, told end to end; do not switch projects between parts.
Answer each part in 1–3 minutes when asked as a follow-up.

## Worked solution

### One project, end to end

No personal experience is supplied. Here is a **single illustrative project** for a 10–15 minute rehearsal; replace it with a project you actually owned and its measured evidence. A team of five builds a real-time feature service for risk decisions. My scope is the feature update protocol, online read API and migration; others own model training and the stream platform.

**Context and difficulty (2 minutes).** The business needs fresh, reproducible features without slowing risk decisions. Multiple event sources duplicate, reorder and occasionally omit updates. Set launch targets before coding: for example, p99 feature read below 20 ms and no unexplained divergence in a replay audit. Those are assumed targets, not reported results.

**Three trade-offs (4 minutes).** First, use event-time windows with a bounded lateness policy instead of processing-time-only windows: this improves reproducibility but requires late-event correction. Second, materialize online values in a key-value store instead of querying the warehouse: this lowers latency but introduces train/serve skew risk. Share transformation definitions and compare replayed offline values to online outputs. Third, use versioned idempotent updates rather than exactly-once claims: an event ID prevents duplicate contribution and a monotonic aggregate version prevents stale overwrites; a transactional boundary must cover both dedup and update.

**Scope (2 minutes).** Ship a small set of high-value numeric features for one model, shadow against the existing batch pipeline, then migrate one tenant at a time. Defer a self-service expression language and multi-region active/active writes. Keep rollback, deletion semantics, auth and reconciliation in version one because they protect correctness, not convenience.

**Measurement (2 minutes).** Compare to the existing pipeline on the same entity/event samples. Measure feature freshness, mismatch rate, p99 under matched load, operational incidents and model decision quality. Report actual baseline and outcome values with time windows and denominators. Do not claim an increase in model quality from lower latency alone.

**Driving agreement (3 minutes).** A staff engineer favors direct stream-to-cache writes to ship quickly; I worry about duplicate counting after retries. Write the invariant together, run a crash-between-dedup-and-update test, and compare a transactional update with the simpler path. Agree on a small atomic operation and defer broader machinery. Document the rejected alternative and the load level at which to revisit it. Give teammates credit for their objections and implementation.

In hindsight, specify late-event and deletion contracts before choosing storage. A concrete follow-up example is a duplicate purchase event: accepting it twice doubles a rolling-spend feature and can change a risk decision. Explain exactly where your implementation prevents that, then describe what failure still requires reconciliation.
