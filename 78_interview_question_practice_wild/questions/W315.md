## Question

Tell the story of a time you strongly disagreed with a technical or product decision made by your team or a more senior person. Interviewers use this question to see how you handle conflict: whether you raise concerns with evidence, stay respectful, and move the work forward whatever the outcome.

What to deliver

Prepare a spoken answer of about three minutes built on one real situation from your own work. Answer all of these parts, in order:
1. Situation: What was the decision, who made it, and why did you disagree? State your role and what was at stake.
2. Evidence: What facts, data or experiments did you gather to test your own view? Did you consider that you might be wrong?
3. Action: Whom did you talk to, in which order, and how did you phrase the disagreement? What alternative did you propose?
4. Resolution: What was decided in the end? If the decision went against you, how did you commit to it? If it went your way, how did you treat the people who disagreed?
5. Result and learning: What happened afterward, and what would you do differently? Describe results honestly; do not invent numbers.
Example of a strong opening

"Our team planned to launch a ranking model after an evaluation that only used overall accuracy. I owned the evaluation pipeline, not the launch decision, and I was worried that the model did much worse on a small user segment. I computed per-segment metrics before raising the concern."

The opening names the decision, the speaker's role and the concern, and it shows evidence-gathering before escalation.

Example of a weak opening

"I disagreed with my manager, so I told everyone the plan was wrong and we argued until he gave in."

It has no evidence, no alternative, and no collaborative resolution.

### Constraints

Use one concrete story, not a general philosophy of disagreement.
Spend about 60% of the time on your actions, because the interviewer is evaluating your behavior.
Use "I" for your own actions and "we" for team outcomes.
Do not blame colleagues; describe their reasoning fairly.

## Worked solution

### A three-minute illustrative story

No personal history is supplied. The following is an **example**, not a claim about the reader. Replace it with a real situation and honest evidence; spend most of the spoken answer on your actions.

**Situation.** “Our team planned to launch a ranking change using overall accuracy. I owned evaluation, not the launch decision. I worried that an overall improvement hid a regression for new users, while the lead reasonably wanted to avoid delaying a promised release.”

**Evidence.** “I reran the same held-out examples by tenure, checked label counts and uncertainty, and inspected mistakes. I also checked whether the small slice was too noisy to support my concern. It turned out the cold-start feature fallback differed from training; the evidence was a reproducible input/feature trace, not just a worse aggregate score.”

**Action.** “I first met the lead privately, describing the shared goal: ship without surprising new users. I showed the failing cases and asked what evidence would change either view. I proposed a bounded feature-fallback fix plus a canary, rather than blocking all launch work. Then I included the feature owner and product partner so the schedule and user impact were visible. I wrote the alternatives and release gates in the decision note.”

**Resolution.** “We agreed to retain the old path for cold users while canarying the new model elsewhere, then fix and evaluate the fallback. I implemented the regression test and supported the rollout. I credited the lead's scope suggestion; I did not present the decision as winning an argument.” If the real outcome went against you, describe the evidence you documented, the escalation boundary if necessary, and how you committed to the accepted safe plan.

**Result and learning.** “The failing cases no longer reproduced and the release had an explicit slice gate. I do not have an isolated business-impact number for this change. I learned to agree slice expectations before model selection instead of discovering them at launch.” In a real story, give actual measured results and limitations. Do not invent a percentage merely to make the story sound stronger.
