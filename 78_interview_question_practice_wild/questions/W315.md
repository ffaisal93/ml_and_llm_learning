## Question

Tell the story of a time you strongly disagreed with a technical or product decision made by your team or a more senior person. Interviewers use this question to see how you handle conflict: whether you raise concerns with evidence, stay respectful, and move the work forward whatever the outcome.

What to deliver

Prepare a spoken answer of about three minutes built on one real situation from your own work. Answer all of these parts, in order:
1. Situation: What was the decision, who made it, and why did you disagree? State your role and what was at stake.
2. Evidence: What facts, data or experiments did you gather to test your own view? Did you consider that you might be wrong?
3. Action: Whom did you talk to, in which order, and how did you phrase the disagreement? What alternative did you propose?
4. Resolution: What was decided in the end? If the decision went against you, how did you commit to it? If it went your way, how did you treat the people who disagreed?
5. Result and learning: What happened afterward, and what would you do differently? Describe results honestly; do not invent numbers.
Example of a strong opening

"Our team planned to launch a ranking model after an evaluation that only used overall accuracy. I owned the evaluation pipeline, not the launch decision, and I was worried that the model did much worse on a small user segment. I computed per-segment metrics before raising the concern."

The opening names the decision, the speaker's role and the concern, and it shows evidence-gathering before escalation.

Example of a weak opening

"I disagreed with my manager, so I told everyone the plan was wrong and we argued until he gave in."

It has no evidence, no alternative, and no collaborative resolution.

### Constraints

Use one concrete story, not a general philosophy of disagreement.
Spend about 60% of the time on your actions, because the interviewer is evaluating your behavior.
Use "I" for your own actions and "we" for team outcomes.
Do not blame colleagues; describe their reasoning fairly.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

Make the disputed assumptions testable and connect the decision to the shared objective.

1. Restate the other person's concern fairly and identify whether the disagreement concerns facts, constraints or preferences.
2. Propose a small experiment or decision table with agreed success criteria, cost and deadline.
3. Document the decision, support execution after it is made and revisit only when meaningful new evidence appears.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Give evidence for a decision, not a polished but untestable story

Use an actual experience and separate your responsibility from the team's. State the problem and constraint, the alternative you considered, your action and the observable result. If you do not have a defensible metric, say what evidence you do have instead of inventing a percentage.

For a technical project, explain the data/split, baseline, hardest failure and the experiment that changed your mind. For conflict or feedback, represent the other person's concern fairly and show how you tested assumptions or changed behavior. “I won the argument” is not the project outcome.

For a research claim, distinguish mechanism from evidence. A bigger training dataset or compute budget can confound an architecture comparison. Ask which ablation separates the proposed explanation from a plausible alternative and whether uncertainty is large relative to the claimed gain.

A useful spoken structure is problem → constraint → decision → evidence → limitation → next step. Keep a longer derivation or failure trace ready for follow-up rather than hiding weak reasoning behind jargon. A motivation/timeline question needs a direct truthful answer, not a generic success story; protect confidential details and never fabricate offers or deadlines.

### Work a small example

A debate over retrieval complexity can become a measured comparison of answer quality, p95 latency and maintenance cost on a fixed query set.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Describing a colleague as incompetent or presenting “I won the argument” as the result.

### Explain it back

Close the answer. State the main invariant or equation, work the example with changed inputs, and explain which assumption would make your solution fail. For a design, walk through a retry or partial failure; for a derivative, check a shape and a finite-difference case.
