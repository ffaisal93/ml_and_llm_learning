## Worked solution

1. Explain logistic regression from scores to gradients. Logistic regression models binary probability with a linear score passed through a sigmoid.
2. Compute z=xᵀw+b and p=sigmoid(z); p/(1-p)=exp(z), so the log odds are linear.
3. Fit weights by minimizing binary cross-entropy, preferably calculated from logits for stability.
4. The score gradient is p-y, giving weight gradient Xᵀ(p-y)/N under mean reduction. Choose the classification threshold using validation costs and metrics.
5. Diagnose high bias versus high variance. Use training and validation behavior to distinguish insufficient fitting from poor generalization.
6. If both errors remain high, check the pipeline and optimization first, then consider inadequate features or capacity.
7. If training error is low but validation error is high, check leakage-free splitting, distribution shift and overfitting.
8. Use learning curves and controlled changes: more data, regularization, simpler models or better features depending on the diagnosis.
9. Compare L1 and L2 regularization. Both penalize large coefficients, but their geometry produces different coefficient patterns.
10. Add lambda*sum(abs(w)) for L1 or a stated lambda-scaled squared norm for L2.
11. L1 can produce exact zeros with suitable optimization, aiding sparse selection; L2 usually shrinks coefficients smoothly and stabilizes correlated predictors.
12. Standardize features using training statistics, tune lambda on validation data and state whether the intercept is penalized.
13. Compare random forests and gradient boosting. Random forests average diversified trees; boosting adds models sequentially to improve an objective.
14. A forest typically trains trees on bootstrapped samples with feature randomness, reducing variance through averaging.
15. Boosting fits successive updates informed by current errors or loss gradients; learning rate, tree depth and stopping determine its capacity.
16. Compare time-aware validation, calibration, inference cost and sensitivity to hyperparameters rather than selecting by reputation.
17. Why use AdamW, warmup and gradient clipping? They address different training problems: adaptive update scale, early-step instability and unusually large gradients.
18. Adam maintains first and second moment estimates, with bias correction after zero initialization.
19. AdamW applies decoupled weight decay rather than treating decay exactly as another adaptively scaled loss gradient.
20. Warm up learning rate over initial steps and clip the global gradient norm when appropriate. Inspect logs and ablations; these are tuning tools, not correctness fixes.
21. Implement k-means with convergence and empty clusters. Alternate assigning points to their closest centers and moving each center to the mean of its assigned points.
22. Initialize k distinct data indices under an explicit random seed, requiring 1<=k<=N.
23. Compute squared distances, assign closest centers and recompute non-empty means. For an empty cluster, retain its center or explicitly reseed it.
24. Stop on a center-shift tolerance or iteration cap, then recompute labels for the returned centers. Compare objective values, not center ordering, across implementations.

### Small example / sanity check

A logit of 0 means p=0.5; a logit of log(3) means p=0.75 and odds 3:1.

### Common trap

Calling sigmoid a hard threshold, or assuming a threshold of 0.5 is optimal for every cost structure.
