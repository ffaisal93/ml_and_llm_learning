## Question

You are joining a company that builds AI software for commercial insurance underwriters. Explain how commercial underwriting works, from the moment a broker sends a new risk to the moment a policy is bound. Then decide which steps of that workflow an AI product should automate first, which steps it should not try to replace, and how you would know the product is working. Finish with the questions you would ask an underwriter before building anything.

The question checks whether you understand the customer's workflow and business problem, not only the engineering.

What to deliver
A spoken walkthrough of the underwriting workflow in five to seven steps, each in one or two sentences.
A recommendation: the first step to automate, with the reason, and one step you would deliberately leave to the underwriter, with the reason.
The metric you would use to judge the product, stated in the customer's terms.
Three questions for an underwriter that the company's website could not answer.
What a strong answer covers
1. The workflow: submission from a broker, clearance, appetite triage, risk assessment, rating and pricing, terms, quote and bind.
2. Where the value is: which steps are mostly data handling and which are expert judgement, and why turnaround time matters commercially.
3. The hard sub-problem: why extracting data from broker documents (applications, loss runs, schedules of values) is difficult and why its errors are expensive.
4. The limits: why replacing the underwriter's risk judgement meets resistance, and how pricing regulation pushes toward systems that explain their output.
5. Measurement: a customer unit such as time from submission to quote, submissions handled per underwriter per day, or the share of declines issued the same day.
6. Curiosity: specific questions about the customer's worst step, where a human stays in the loop, and what the customer measures.

### Examples

"Walk me through how a commercial insurance submission becomes a policy." The answer lists the steps in order and says which ones are data handling and which are judgement.
"Which part of underwriting would you automate first, and why?" One defended choice, for example triage before full extraction, with the condition that would change your mind.

### Constraints

1. Describe commercial (business) insurance, not personal car or home insurance.
2. You do not need exact regulations, but you must state that pricing decisions are regulated and what that implies for the system.
3. Give one recommendation with a reason; a list of everything that could be automated is not an answer.
4. Keep the walkthrough to about three minutes spoken, leaving time for discussion.
Follow-up 1: what would make it fail

What would make this product fail even if the models were accurate?

Follow-up 2: training on past decisions

A colleague proposes training a model on historical accept and decline decisions. What is the risk?

Follow-up 3: what you do not know

What do you not yet know about this industry, and how would you find out?

## Worked solution

### A three-minute commercial-underwriting walkthrough

1. A broker submits a business risk: applications, business description, loss runs and schedules of locations/values. Intake associates documents with the correct applicant and request.
2. Clearance checks duplicates, existing relationships and which underwriter/market owns the submission; it prevents conflicting work and quoting.
3. Appetite triage checks whether industry, geography, size and requested coverage fit the insurer's current appetite, then requests missing essentials or routes an obvious nonfit.
4. Risk assessment reviews exposures, prior losses, controls and external information. Documents often disagree on dates, units, subsidiaries and loss status, so extracted fields need source provenance and human verification.
5. Rating/pricing estimates expected loss and expenses within applicable rules and underwriting authority; terms include limits, deductibles, exclusions and conditions. Pricing decisions are regulated, so the system needs explainable, versioned inputs and an approval trail rather than opaque autonomous edits.
6. The underwriter issues a quote; broker/customer negotiation resolves terms, subjectivities and approvals. Binding accepts the agreed coverage and produces policy records only after required conditions are met.

**Build first:** a human-reviewed intake/triage assistant that extracts a small set of appetite fields with document/page evidence and highlights missing/conflicting values. It reduces repetitive handling and speeds clear declines/routing without taking over judgment. This recommendation changes if observation shows clearance, not triage, dominates delay. Do not attempt autonomous risk acceptance/pricing first: errors can create expensive coverage commitments, and underwriters must retain authority and reasoning.

Measure submission-to-first-useful-response/quote time and submissions handled per underwriter per day, with rework, extraction error on high-impact fields and inappropriate-decline rate as guardrails. Model field accuracy is not the customer outcome. A perfectly accurate model can still fail through poor workflow integration, duplicate data entry, distrust, no evidence trail or incentives that reward quote volume over profitable business.

Historical accept/decline labels encode old appetite, capacity, underwriter discretion and broker selection; declined risks usually lack insured loss outcomes. Training directly on them can reproduce bias and obsolete policy rather than estimate true risk. Separate routing-policy imitation from risk prediction and obtain representative expert review.

Ask an underwriter: “Show me the last submission that stalled and where you spent the time”; “Which fields or decisions must you verify personally, and what evidence convinces you?”; “Which team metric would make you keep this tool after a month, and what error would make you stop?” State what you do not know—line-specific authority, broker handoffs, document variation—and learn through shadowing real submissions and checking reviewed cases, not guessing exact regulations.
