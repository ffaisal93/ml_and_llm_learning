## Question

Beat the Odds — Probability Test

Answer a timed set of multiple-choice probability questions, choosing for each the option closest to the true value.

### Format

1. About 30 questions, roughly 90 seconds each. A question can be skipped, but you cannot go back to it.
2. Each question has about five numeric options; select the one closest to the exact answer. Fast estimation is usually enough.
3. Wrong answers are penalised, so a blind guess has negative expected value.
4. Topics: dice, coin and card probability; expected value; random walks on polygons and grids (hitting and return times); gambler's ruin; combinatorics; normal-approximation estimates.
Practice questions

For each question, give the probability or expected value.
1. A fair die is thrown twice. Probability that the second throw differs from the first.
2. A shuffled 52-card deck has its top 10 cards discarded. Probability that the new top card is red.
3. Two dice are thrown. Probability that the sum is 11 or 12.
4. A coin is flipped 3 times. Probability that all three outcomes are the same.

```text
5. 61 coins are placed at random into 15 boxes. Probability that some box holds more than 4 coins.
```

6. A bag holds 10 red, 10 yellow and 10 blue balls. Balls are drawn uniformly at random without replacement until one colour is used up (only two colours remain in the bag). Expected number of balls left in the bag.
7. A die is rolled repeatedly, keeping a running sum, until the sum exceeds 100. Probability that the last roll was a 2.
8. Two particles start at opposite vertices of a regular octagon. Every second each particle independently flips a fair coin and moves one edge clockwise or anticlockwise. Expected number of seconds until they are on the same vertex, and the corresponding number of coin flips (two per second).
9. A particle sits on a vertex of a regular decagon. Every second a die is rolled: faces 1-2 move it clockwise, faces 3-4 anticlockwise, faces 5-6 leave it in place. Expected number of seconds until it first reaches the vertex opposite its start.
10. A particle starts at lattice point (5, 5) of the grid of points (x, y) with 0 &lt;= x, y &lt;= 10. Each step it moves up, down, left or right with probability 1/4 each. Expected number of steps until it first reaches a point with x or y equal to 0 or 10.
11. Start with USD 10 and wager USD 1 per independent round, stopping at USD 0 or USD 20. Find the probability of reaching USD 20 for win probabilities (a) 0.5 and (b) 0.6.
12. Six fair coins are flipped. Probability of (a) more heads than tails, (b) at most 3 heads.
13. A machine outputs integers uniformly from 1 to 10. You and a friend each add two outputs. Probability that the two sums are equal.
14. An integer is chosen uniformly from 1 to 1,000,000. Probability that it contains the digit 7 at least once.
Output
For each question, a number (probability or expectation) as an exact fraction or a decimal to 3 significant figures, precise enough to pick the closest option. Questions 8, 11 and 12 have two values each.

### Constraints

About 90 seconds per question; a calculator and scratch paper are usually allowed.
Recognise the family (counting, symmetry, first-step analysis, closed forms) rather than computing from scratch.

## Worked solution

### Selection strategy with a penalty

Answer all fourteen provided practice questions below; the remaining questions in the described roughly thirty-question test are not supplied and cannot be reconstructed. If a correct choice earns a and an incorrect one loses b, guessing with assessed correctness probability p has expected score pa−(1−p)b. Answer when p>b/(a+b), otherwise skip when allowed. The actual penalty and options are missing, so no universal numeric guessing threshold is justified; eliminating options can make a question worthwhile.

### Nontrivial expected values, derived

For part 6 let E(a,b,c) be expected balls left at the stopping time from remaining counts. If any count is zero, stop immediately with E=a+b+c. Otherwise

$$E(a,b,c)=\frac{aE(a-1,b,c)+bE(a,b-1,c)+cE(a,b,c-1)}{a+b+c}.$$

Exact rational dynamic programming over 11³ states yields E(10,10,10)=310/77. There is also a compact tail-sum check: after the first color is exhausted, the residual suffix of a random 30-ball ordering contains at most two colors. For k≥1, the probability the last k positions omit at least one color is [3 C(20,k)−3 C(10,k)]/C(30,k), with impossible binomials zero. Summing k=1..20 gives the same expected remaining suffix length, 310/77. This is **remaining**, not number drawn; expected draws are 30−310/77.

For part 7, positive die increments visit each partial sum at most once. Set u(0)=1, u(s)=Σ[d=1..6,d≤s]u(s−d)/6. The last roll can be 2 only from sum 99 or 100, hence [u(99)+u(100)]/6. The threshold crossing favors large last rolls, so 1/6 is wrong. The numerical value is extremely close to 2/21, but the recurrence, not an unsupported limiting replacement, defines the finite-threshold answer.

For part 8 the relative four-cycle state has stay probability 1/2 and ±1 probability 1/4 each. By symmetry h1=h3 and h0=0. Equations h1=1+h1/2+h2/4 and h2=1+h2/2+h1/2 give h1=6,h2=8. Each elapsed second consumes two flips.

For part 10 write h(x,y)=1+[h(x−1,y)+h(x+1,y)+h(x,y−1)+h(x,y+1)]/4 for interior x,y=1..9, with zero boundary. Solving this finite linear system gives the stated central value; a one-dimensional ruin formula does not apply directly to square exit. The linked checker uses exact recurrence/enumeration where practical and a linear solve with residual verification for the grid, rather than a guessed nice fraction.
