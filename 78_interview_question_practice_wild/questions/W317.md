## Question

You are talking with the manager of a team you may join. The conversation decides whether your background fits the open role and the team's current needs. It is a two-way evaluation: you are expected to present yourself concisely and to ask informed questions.

What to prepare
1. Background pitch (5-10 minutes). Walk through your career at resume-highlight level: roles, scope, and the thread that connects them to this role. Prepare a 30-second version as well.
2. Project summaries. Describe two or three projects at elevator-pitch level: the problem, your specific contribution, and the measurable outcome.
3. Questions for the manager (3-5). Ask substantive questions about the team's structure, current roadmap and open problems, the scope of the role, and the growth path from your level to the next.
4. Motivation. Give a concise answer to "why are you looking" and "what would make this role compelling".

### Constraints

The conversation lasts about 45 minutes, so the pitch, the projects and your questions must all fit.
The manager decides whether the background fits the specific needs of the open role, so tie every answer to the role's scope.

## Worked solution

### Fit the pitch, projects and questions into forty-five minutes

No resume or job description is supplied, so all examples here are **illustrative**. A 30-second pitch can be: “My work has moved from backend reliability toward ML serving. I have owned the data/serving boundary and production rollout on small teams. I am looking for a role where I can take that ownership further, and I would like to understand whether your current priority is model quality, platform reliability or both.” Replace every claim with your actual background.

Expand to a 5–10 minute pitch by covering two or three career transitions, the scope at each and why each prepares you for this role. Avoid a chronological inventory of every tool. Reserve roughly 15 minutes for two or three concise project summaries and follow-ups, 10–15 for role discussion, and five for your questions; let the manager redirect.

For each project use problem→my contribution→outcome→relevance. Example summaries are: an idempotent event-consumer migration (I owned protocol/rollout; team owned the product), a ranking experiment (I owned labels/loss/evaluation) and a serving incident fix (I localized queueing and validated a bounded admission policy). Supply real baselines, outcome windows and scope; where no reliable metric exists, name the evidence rather than invent one. Offer deeper detail only on the project most relevant to the team's need.

Ask four substantive questions: “Which unresolved problem would I own in the first six months?”; “How are decisions split among this team, product and platform partners?”; “What roadmap trade-off is hardest right now, and why?”; “What observable scope distinguishes my target level from the next, and how have people grown into it?” Ask about on-call/operational ownership if relevant.

For motivation, state a truthful positive goal, for example broader end-to-end responsibility or deeper serving work, and the conditions that make the role compelling. Do not invent a current employer problem or a competing offer. Close by checking the fit against the manager's actual priorities, not assuming a familiar company title guarantees the desired scope.
