## Question

You are talking with the manager of a team you may join. The conversation decides whether your background fits the open role and the team's current needs. It is a two-way evaluation: you are expected to present yourself concisely and to ask informed questions.

What to prepare
1. Background pitch (5-10 minutes). Walk through your career at resume-highlight level: roles, scope, and the thread that connects them to this role. Prepare a 30-second version as well.
2. Project summaries. Describe two or three projects at elevator-pitch level: the problem, your specific contribution, and the measurable outcome.
3. Questions for the manager (3-5). Ask substantive questions about the team's structure, current roadmap and open problems, the scope of the role, and the growth path from your level to the next.
4. Motivation. Give a concise answer to "why are you looking" and "what would make this role compelling".

### Constraints

The conversation lasts about 45 minutes, so the pitch, the projects and your questions must all fit.
The manager decides whether the background fits the specific needs of the open role, so tie every answer to the role's scope.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

Connect your real experience to the role through a small number of relevant capabilities and outcomes.

1. Start with your current focus and the kinds of problems you solve.
2. Choose one or two concrete projects, naming your role, difficult decision and measured result.
3. Finish with why the target work fits your interests and what you want to contribute, without inventing credentials or metrics.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Give evidence for a decision, not a polished but untestable story

Use an actual experience and separate your responsibility from the team's. State the problem and constraint, the alternative you considered, your action and the observable result. If you do not have a defensible metric, say what evidence you do have instead of inventing a percentage.

For a technical project, explain the data/split, baseline, hardest failure and the experiment that changed your mind. For conflict or feedback, represent the other person's concern fairly and show how you tested assumptions or changed behavior. “I won the argument” is not the project outcome.

For a research claim, distinguish mechanism from evidence. A bigger training dataset or compute budget can confound an architecture comparison. Ask which ablation separates the proposed explanation from a plausible alternative and whether uncertainty is large relative to the claimed gain.

A useful spoken structure is problem → constraint → decision → evidence → limitation → next step. Keep a longer derivation or failure trace ready for follow-up rather than hiding weak reasoning behind jargon. A motivation/timeline question needs a direct truthful answer, not a generic success story; protect confidential details and never fabricate offers or deadlines.

### Work a small example

An MLE introduction can connect data-quality work, reliable serving and one measured deployment result instead of listing every library used.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Reciting the entire resume chronologically or making claims you cannot defend under follow-up.

### Explain it back

Close the answer. State the main invariant or equation, work the example with changed inputs, and explain which assumption would make your solution fail. For a design, walk through a retry or partial failure; for a derivative, check a shape and a finite-difference case.
