## Question

Design a data and training system that converts a large, noisy collection of screen observations and user actions into useful supervision for a multimodal UI-navigation agent. The corpus mixes successful, failed, accidental, and incomplete trajectories, so the system must estimate quality without discarding informative failures or reinforcing its own model errors.

Inputs and constraints

```text
100 million trajectories, averaging 20 screen frames at 250 KB each plus action and UI-tree metadata; raw storage is therefore about 500 TB before replication and derived features.
10,000 human-verified seed trajectories spanning common tasks and failure modes.
```

A weekly labeling budget of 5,000 trajectories and a per-iteration compute budget of 2,000 GPU-hours (7.2 million GPU-seconds) shared by scoring, training, and rollouts.
One training-and-refresh iteration per week. Rules and metadata checks run on CPU over all 100 million trajectories. A small thumbnail encoder may score at most 10% of the corpus (10 million trajectories) per iteration, and a vision-language model (VLM) may score at most 200,000 trajectories per iteration.
Planning assumptions for the estimate: the small encoder processes 1,000 frames per GPU-second, and the VLM needs 5 GPU-seconds per trajectory.
Actions use a typed schema: tap(x, y), type(target, text), swipe(start, end), back, and wait. Every action links pre-action and post-action observations.

### Requirements

1. Define ingestion, privacy controls, deduplication, versioning, and a valid-trajectory schema. Distinguish malformed data, unsuccessful attempts, and coherent successful paths.
2. Combine deterministic validators, learned quality models, weak supervision, and selective human review. State how thresholds are calibrated and how label noise is audited.
3. Attach or generate instructions for useful trajectories, measure relevance and novelty, and mix curated, weakly labeled, failed, and synthetic examples during training.
4. Design a weekly loop that trains the navigation policy, evaluates it on frozen tasks, uses it to rescore or relabel candidates, and prevents confirmation bias.
5. Define Plan Coherence Score as a calibrated estimate that the ordered actions are consistent with the instruction and observed state changes. Explain how it is trained and evaluated rather than treating it as an unexplained model output.
6. Use metamorphic tests. Transform a trajectory in a way with a known expected effect, such as deleting a required action, perturbing a tap outside its target, or paraphrasing the instruction. Then verify that the validator's decision changes or stays stable as expected.
7. Cover replay or simulation, retries and alternative rollouts, active-learning selection, data lineage, failure analysis, and safe rollback.
Target metrics
At least 90% precision at 70% recall for rejecting invalid trajectories on a frozen, human-labeled audit set.
Improve task success on a held-out UI/task suite without regressing any safety-critical task family by more than 1 percentage point.
Report coverage as the fraction of a fixed held-out screen-and-action inventory observed in accepted data, not as a fraction of an unknowable set of all possible screens.
Keep every accepted example traceable to source version, filters, labels, model version, and reviewer decisions.
Deliverable

Present the architecture, storage and compute estimates, data schemas, scoring and sampling strategy, training loop, evaluation design, monitoring, privacy controls, and trade-offs. Include a GPU-hour budget table that splits the 2,000 GPU-hours among scoring, training, and rollouts, and show the labeling budget in use.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

A recorded trajectory is not automatically a valid demonstration of the desired behavior.

1. Define success criteria, allowed observation/action fields and privacy filtering; deduplicate by task or session.
2. Filter incomplete, corrupted or failed trajectories, then label steps or outcomes under an explicit policy. Preserve ordering and provenance within the internal dataset without including private content unnecessarily.
3. Split by task, user or time as appropriate, and test whether a small clean subset improves a fixed evaluation before scaling collection.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Establish what the number actually estimates

Write the population, unit of analysis, outcome window and denominator next to the metric. A per-user mean and a per-impression mean answer different questions. A global mean over unequal batches is

$$
\bar\ell=\frac{\sum_b\sum_{i\in b}\ell_i}{\sum_b n_b},
$$

not generally the unweighted mean of batch means. Workers should reduce numerators and denominators separately when reporting that metric.

Use a comparison with matched examples, mature labels and unchanged definitions. For uncertainty, resample independent units; repeated events within a user or prompt are not automatically independent. A confidence interval measures sampling uncertainty under assumptions, not protection against leaked data or an inappropriate target.

Calibration asks whether predictions around probability $p$ are correct about fraction $p$ of the time. It is not the same as accuracy. A reliability plot should show bin populations, and a calibration-error formula must say whether bins or examples receive equal weight.

When quality regresses, trace data availability, feature freshness, label delays and cohort mix before automatically retraining. For example, a fraud model may look worse on immature labels even when its true behavior has not changed. Report missing/time-out outputs explicitly: dropping them from the denominator can turn an unreliable system into an apparently perfect one.

### Work a small example

A successful final screen may follow many accidental clicks; copying every action as equally good supervision teaches noise.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Using future success metadata as an input feature that would not exist during live action selection.
