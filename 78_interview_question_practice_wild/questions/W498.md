## Question

Capital One Power Day case (Strategy & Data Analytics rotation), 45 minutes. Opens with conceptual questions about an amusement park business, then builds park profitability under different operating assumptions.

Phase 1: warm-up — define the levers of park profitability (ticket revenue, per-visitor in-park spend, operating costs, capacity constraints, seasonality).
Phase 2: build the P&L for a specified scenario (e.g. a 5,000-capacity park running 200 days/year at 70% utilization) — revenue, variable cost, fixed cost, net profit; mental math expected.
Phase 3: sensitivity / scenario analysis — e.g. "what happens if utilization drops 10%?" or "what is the break-even ticket price?" Identify the most impactful lever.

## Worked solution

### Build visits and contribution before profit

Capacity of 5,000 visitors/day × 200 days × 70% utilization gives 700,000 annual visits; repeat visitors count as visits. Because prices and costs are not supplied, use this explicitly illustrative scenario: ticket 50 USD, in-park revenue 20 USD/visit, variable cost 25 USD/visit and fixed annual cost 20 million USD. Revenue is 49 million USD, variable cost 17.5 million, contribution 31.5 million and operating profit 11.5 million. Define whether fixed costs include depreciation; tax, interest and capex are excluded unless added explicitly.

Profit is $\Pi=CDu(P+S-v)-F$. Utilization dropping **10 percentage points** from 70% to 60% gives 600K visits and profit of 7 million USD, a 4.5 million USD decline. A **10% relative** drop means 63%, 630K visits and profit of 8.35 million USD, a 3.15 million USD decline. Clarifying the phrase avoids a common case error.

Break-even ticket price at the original attendance is $P^*=F/(CDu)+v-S=20M/700K+25-20\approx\$33.57$. Break-even utilization is $F/[CD(P+S-v)]\approx44.44\%$. These are static calculations; reducing ticket price may change demand and in-park spending, which requires an elasticity assumption rather than holding everything fixed unnoticed.

A one-percentage-point utilization increase adds 10,000 visits × 45 USD contribution, or 450,000 USD within capacity. A one-dollar ticket or variable-cost change affects 700,000 visits, about 700,000 USD. Compare feasible magnitudes and demand/quality effects, not derivatives in different units. Seasonality, weather, staffing, queues, safety, maintenance and peak-day capacity can make average utilization misleading. Segment weekdays/peak days and test pricing/bundling against margin and guest experience. Obtain actual cost, pass/repeat-visit and investment assumptions before using the illustration as a recommendation.
