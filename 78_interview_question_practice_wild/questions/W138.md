## Question

Deep Learning Fundamentals: Optimization, Drift, Calibration

Deep Learning / Research screens can be pure fundamentals: gradient descent, batch vs SGD, convexity, sharp vs flat minima, bias-variance, calibration, model drift, and business framing of ML quality problems.

### Requirements

How gradient descent works; role of learning rate.

Which loss surfaces guarantee convergence to a global minimum.
Full-batch vs mini-batch vs stochastic gradient descent: advantages and disadvantages.
Bias vs variance and how to reduce each.
Why small batches can escape sharp / shallow local minima.
Generalization gap: train distribution vs population distribution.
Why flatter minima may generalize better than sharp minima.
Confidence calibration: definition and common methods.
Model drift: definition and detection.
How to translate a customer / business scenario into a measurable ML problem.
Notes

The expected answers are conceptual but precise.

Convex loss surfaces are the clean case where any local minimum is global.
SGD has noisier gradient estimates than full-batch GD; that noise can help escape some sharp or shallow basins.
Full-batch uses the exact empirical gradient but may converge to sharper minima and is often less scalable.
Generalization gap appears when training loss and population loss diverge; flat minima are less sensitive to small distribution shifts.
Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities match empirical frequencies. Temperature scaling, Platt scaling, isotonic regression, and reliability diagrams are standard tools.
Drift detection can monitor feature distribution shift, prediction distribution shift, calibration decay, performance on delayed labels, and business KPI deltas.

## Worked solution

### Gradient descent and convergence claims

For empirical objective $L(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_i\ell_i(\theta)$, gradient descent uses $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta_t\nabla L(\theta_t)$. The learning rate controls step length: too small is slow, too large can oscillate/diverge. For $L(\theta)=a\theta^2/2$, the update is $(1-\eta a)\theta$, converging when $0<\eta<2/a$. A differentiable convex objective has no nonglobal local minima, but convergence still needs suitable steps and regularity/existence of a minimizer. For L-smooth convex objectives, standard suitably bounded steps give global objective convergence; strong convexity yields faster linear rates. Logistic loss on separable data can have an unattained infimum, so convexity alone does not guarantee finite converged parameters. General neural networks are nonconvex and GD has no universal global-optimum guarantee.

### Full batch, minibatch and stochastic noise

Full batch computes the exact finite-training-set gradient, is stable but expensive per step and can be memory inefficient. SGD uses one random example; minibatches average a subset for better hardware use and lower variance. Under independent draws, gradient-estimate variance roughly shrinks as 1/batch_size (finite sampling corrections apply); shuffling and correlated examples complicate that approximation. Small-batch noise can help leave saddles or shallow/sharp basins when it has a useful component, but does not guarantee escape or better generalization. Compare equal compute and tuned schedules.

### Bias, variance and generalization

At fixed x for squared prediction loss, expected test error = irreducible noise + squared estimator bias + training-set variance, with a fresh label independent of training. Richer features/capacity or weaker regularization can reduce approximation bias; more independent data, averaging and stronger regularization can reduce variance, often trading one against another. These are tendencies, and the additive form does not transfer unchanged to hard classification error.

The generalization gap is population expected loss minus training empirical loss of the learned model; a validation estimate also contains sampling error and distribution shift. A flat basin is less sensitive to small **parameter** perturbations and can correlate with robustness under particular assumptions. Flatness depends on parameterization (rescaling equivalent networks changes curvature), so it is not a universal explanation or proof of robustness to input/distribution changes. Full-batch training is not inevitably sharp or poor.

### Calibration, drift and an operational target

Calibration means $P(Y=1\mid\hat p=p)=p$: among cases scored .8, about 80% should be positive. Use held-out reliability diagrams with counts plus Brier/log loss. Temperature scaling learns a positive logit divisor, Platt scaling a sigmoid affine score map, and isotonic regression a monotone fit; train these on held-out predictions and test once afterward. Better ranking need not imply better calibration.

Input drift changes P(X), prior drift P(Y), concept drift P(Y|X); broken pipelines and delayed labels can mimic these. Monitor schema/missingness/freshness, feature and score distribution changes, then mature-label performance/calibration by matched cohorts, with alert thresholds and multiple-testing controls. Unlabeled drift is a warning, not proof of lost accuracy. Retraining cannot repair a malformed live feature.

Translate “customers receive poor suggestions” into a decision unit, eligible population and outcome window: for example user-level qualified completion within seven days, with complaint/latency guardrails. Define what actions predictions control, compare a simple baseline, choose leakage-free splits and operating costs, and test the intervention prospectively. A lower loss alone is not the business goal.
