## Question

Deep Learning Fundamentals: Optimization, Drift, Calibration

Deep Learning / Research screens can be pure fundamentals: gradient descent, batch vs SGD, convexity, sharp vs flat minima, bias-variance, calibration, model drift, and business framing of ML quality problems.

Notes

The expected answers are conceptual but precise.

Convex loss surfaces are the clean case where any local minimum is global.
SGD has noisier gradient estimates than full-batch GD; that noise can help escape some sharp or shallow basins.
Full-batch uses the exact empirical gradient but may converge to sharper minima and is often less scalable.
Generalization gap appears when training loss and population loss diverge; flat minima are less sensitive to small distribution shifts.
Calibration asks whether predicted probabilities match empirical frequencies. Temperature scaling, Platt scaling, isotonic regression, and reliability diagrams are standard tools.
Drift detection can monitor feature distribution shift, prediction distribution shift, calibration decay, performance on delayed labels, and business KPI deltas.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

#### Why use AdamW, warmup and gradient clipping?

They address different training problems: adaptive update scale, early-step instability and unusually large gradients.

1. Adam maintains first and second moment estimates, with bias correction after zero initialization.
2. AdamW applies decoupled weight decay rather than treating decay exactly as another adaptively scaled loss gradient.
3. Warm up learning rate over initial steps and clip the global gradient norm when appropriate. Inspect logs and ablations; these are tuning tools, not correctness fixes.

#### Distinguish data drift, concept drift and label delay.

A production metric can change because inputs changed, the input-to-label relationship changed, or the labels are not yet mature.

1. Check pipeline health, feature distributions and missingness before assuming the model changed.
2. When mature labels exist, compare conditional performance and calibration on matched slices and periods.
3. Separate delayed outcome windows from true degradation; use operational proxies cautiously and validate them against final outcomes.

#### Check calibration and choose a decision threshold.

A probability estimate and the action taken from it are separate design choices.

1. Measure reliability on a validation set using bin plots and proper scoring rules such as log loss or Brier score.
2. Fit a calibration transform only on held-out predictions if needed, then choose a threshold using explicit costs or operational capacity.
3. Evaluate the entire calibrated-and-thresholded pipeline on untouched test data and important slices.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Establish what the number actually estimates

Write the population, unit of analysis, outcome window and denominator next to the metric. A per-user mean and a per-impression mean answer different questions. A global mean over unequal batches is

$$
\bar\ell=\frac{\sum_b\sum_{i\in b}\ell_i}{\sum_b n_b},
$$

not generally the unweighted mean of batch means. Workers should reduce numerators and denominators separately when reporting that metric.

Use a comparison with matched examples, mature labels and unchanged definitions. For uncertainty, resample independent units; repeated events within a user or prompt are not automatically independent. A confidence interval measures sampling uncertainty under assumptions, not protection against leaked data or an inappropriate target.

Calibration asks whether predictions around probability $p$ are correct about fraction $p$ of the time. It is not the same as accuracy. A reliability plot should show bin populations, and a calibration-error formula must say whether bins or examples receive equal weight.

When quality regresses, trace data availability, feature freshness, label delays and cohort mix before automatically retraining. For example, a fraud model may look worse on immature labels even when its true behavior has not changed. Report missing/time-out outputs explicitly: dropping them from the denominator can turn an unreliable system into an apparently perfect one.

### Work a small example

A gradient [3,4] has norm 5. Clipping its norm to 1 yields [0.6,0.8], preserving direction before the optimizer's own transformation.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Using clipping to hide exploding activations, bad data or incorrect loss scaling.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**Distinguish data drift, concept drift and label delay.** A fraud model's observed precision may look low immediately after deployment because confirmed fraud labels arrive weeks later.

Can input distributions stay stable while performance changes? Yes; concept drift can alter P(y|x) without an obvious marginal shift in x.

**Check calibration and choose a decision threshold.** Under ideal calibrated probabilities and fixed costs, predict positive when p>C_FP/(C_FP+C_FN). With costs 1 and 9, the threshold is 0.1.

What happens under prevalence shift? Probability calibration and the best operating point may change.
