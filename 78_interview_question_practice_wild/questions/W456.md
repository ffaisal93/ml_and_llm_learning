## Question

Atlassian's later-stage behavioral loop is usually split into a values round and a management / hiring-manager round. Questions repeatedly target mentorship, helping teammates, feedback, ownership, ambiguity, sudden changes, mistakes, and alignment with Atlassian's five values.

### Requirements

Prepare for a values-focused round and a management / hiring-manager round.
Values prompts include: Have you been a mentor or mentee? Have you helped a team member improve? What does an effective team look like to you? How did you handle feedback? How did you handle a case where you missed the mark?
Management prompts include: Describe a case where you owned the outcome. How did you handle unclear requirements? How did you handle sudden changes (leadership or direction)? Have you helped a teammate become better?
Other repeated themes: pushback, change and improvement, ambiguity, mistakes and recovery, role clarity, and learning.
Notes
The management round can carry heavy leveling weight; candidates have been downleveled even when technical rounds were stronger.
Interviewers keep probing until they get the signal they need. Answer with role, action, conflict, impact, and learning rather than a generic story.

## Worked solution

### Match real behavior to values, then show management scope

Prepare distinct truthful examples; none are supplied, so these are **illustrative rehearsals**. The published values are Open company, no bullshit; Build with heart and balance; Don't #@!% the customer; Play, as a team; Be the change you seek. Their names come from [Atlassian's values page](https://www.atlassian.com/company/values); the mappings below are an interpretation, not an internal grading rubric.

For mentorship, describe agreeing a concrete goal with a junior engineer, giving a small independent component, reviewing decisions and checking whether they can operate it without you. For helping a teammate, an example is pairing on an incident then writing a reusable diagnostic guide, rather than silently taking their task. For feedback/missing the mark, say what you changed: “My reviews arrived too late, so I scheduled an early design checkpoint and checked whether rework decreased.” Use actual evidence, not an invented percentage.

A management story can center on an ambiguous service migration: I owned the rollout; product owned priority and platform owned infrastructure. I wrote the acceptance criteria, cut an optional feature, made stale-data behavior explicit for customers, and escalated a missed dependency with scope/date alternatives. When leadership changed direction, I confirmed the new objective, explained sunk versus reusable work and updated owners rather than defending the old plan merely because I built it. Show a real setback, the recovery and what you learned.

These moments can demonstrate openness through early risk communication, balance through scoped sustainable delivery, customer focus through correctness/rollback, teamwork through shared credit and mentorship, and change through a process fix. Do not name-drop all values in every sentence. A leveling discussion needs actual decisions, organizational boundaries and outcomes, not a generic team-player claim. Keep personal metrics and collaborator credit accurate and prepare a second example when the interviewer probes for a different signal.
