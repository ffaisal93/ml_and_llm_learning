## Question

<pre class="question-text" style="white-space:pre-wrap;font-family:inherit;line-height:1.55">Two customers each buy two cups of coffee from the same shop. Compute the probability that one customer&#x27;s cups are better than the other&#x27;s, under several precise meanings of &quot;better&quot;.

Setup
Customer A buys cups A1, A2; customer B buys cups B1, B2.
Each cup has a quality score. The four scores are independent and identically distributed.
Parts 1 to 4 use a continuous distribution, so two cups never have exactly the same score. Do not assume any particular distribution.
Tasks
1.
Clean sweep. Find the probability that every one of A&#x27;s cups is better than every one of B&#x27;s cups, i.e. min(A1, A2) &gt; max(B1, B2).
2.
Best cup. Find the probability that A&#x27;s best cup is better than B&#x27;s best cup.
3.
Total. Find the probability that A1 + A2 &gt; B1 + B2.
4.
Head-to-head. Compare each of A&#x27;s cups with each of B&#x27;s cups (four comparisons). Give the full distribution of the number of comparisons A wins, and the probability that A wins strictly more than half.
5.
n cups each. Generalise Task 1 to n cups per customer. Give a closed form, and evaluate it for n = 3, n = 5 and n = 10.
6.
Discrete ratings. Now each score is an integer rating drawn uniformly from {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, so ties are possible. Recompute Tasks 1, 2 and 3 as exact fractions (strict inequality wins; give the tie probability for Tasks 2 and 3). Explain why the answers move away from the continuous ones.
Requirements
Justify each continuous answer with a symmetry or counting argument that does not depend on the distribution.
Give exact fractions, not decimals only.
Describe how you would check every answer with a short simulation.</pre>

## Worked solution

1. A customer gets two coffee cups; what is the probability both beat the other customer's two? Under four independent continuous draws, all relative orderings are equally likely and there are no ties.
2. Label one customer's cups A1,A2 and the other's B1,B2, with “beat” meaning larger quality score.
3. The positions occupied by the two A cups are uniformly distributed among choose(4,2)=6 possibilities.
4. Exactly one placement puts both A cups in the top two positions, so the probability is 1/6.

### Small example / sanity check

The reverse event, both B cups beating both A cups, also has probability 1/6; the remaining 2/3 are mixed orderings.

### Common trap

Treating four pairwise comparisons as independent.
