## Question

Two customers each buy two cups of coffee from the same shop. Compute the probability that one customer's cups are better than the other's, under several precise meanings of "better".

### Setup

Customer A buys cups A1, A2; customer B buys cups B1, B2.
Each cup has a quality score. The four scores are independent and identically distributed.
Parts 1 to 4 use a continuous distribution, so two cups never have exactly the same score. Do not assume any particular distribution.
Tasks
1. Clean sweep. Find the probability that every one of A's cups is better than every one of B's cups, i.e. min(A1, A2) &gt; max(B1, B2).
2. Best cup. Find the probability that A's best cup is better than B's best cup.
3. Total. Find the probability that A1 + A2 &gt; B1 + B2.
4. Head-to-head. Compare each of A's cups with each of B's cups (four comparisons). Give the full distribution of the number of comparisons A wins, and the probability that A wins strictly more than half.
5. n cups each. Generalise Task 1 to n cups per customer. Give a closed form, and evaluate it for n = 3, n = 5 and n = 10.
6. Discrete ratings. Now each score is an integer rating drawn uniformly from {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, so ties are possible. Recompute Tasks 1, 2 and 3 as exact fractions (strict inequality wins; give the tie probability for Tasks 2 and 3). Explain why the answers move away from the continuous ones.

### Requirements

Justify each continuous answer with a symmetry or counting argument that does not depend on the distribution.
Give exact fractions, not decimals only.
Describe how you would check every answer with a short simulation.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

Under four independent continuous draws, all relative orderings are equally likely and there are no ties.

1. Label one customer's cups A1,A2 and the other's B1,B2, with “beat” meaning larger quality score.
2. The positions occupied by the two A cups are uniformly distributed among choose(4,2)=6 possibilities.
3. Exactly one placement puts both A cups in the top two positions, so the probability is 1/6.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Name the random experiment and condition on the right event

Write the sample space and independence assumptions before counting. Bayes' rule reverses conditioning:

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0.
$$

A $90\%$ detection rate is not a $90\%$ probability that an alert is genuine. Prevalence and false-positive rate determine the latter. Work expected counts in a hypothetical population when the formula feels abstract.

For waiting-time and random-walk exercises, first-step analysis often gives a solvable recurrence:

$$
h(s)=1+\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s)h(s'),\qquad h(s)=0\text{ at absorbing states}.
$$

Specify what counts as one step: two particles moving once each may cost two coin flips but only one elapsed second. At a forced horizon, all surviving probability mass must be assigned to the final stopping time.

For expected counts, indicator variables and linearity do not require independent indicators. For uncertainty or variance, dependence does matter. Check normalization, limiting cases and a small enumeration. Simulation is useful validation but not a replacement for a requested exact derivation; conversely, a neat-looking fraction should not be asserted merely because a numerical estimate is close to it.

### Work a small example

The reverse event, both B cups beating both A cups, also has probability 1/6; the remaining 2/3 are mixed orderings.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Treating four pairwise comparisons as independent.
