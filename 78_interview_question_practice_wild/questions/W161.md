## Question

Work through the following four derivations in probability and statistics. For each, state your assumptions, write the distributions, constraints and formulas first, and give an exact closed form (not a simulation or approximation).

### Requirements

1. Correlation feasibility. Three random variables x, y, z have all pairwise correlations equal to p. Find the range of p for which such variables exist, using the positive semi-definite condition on the correlation matrix (eigenvalues, determinant, or the cosine-of-angle picture). Then extend the result to n variables with a common pairwise correlation.
2. Order statistics. For n i.i.d. Uniform(0, 1) variables, compute the covariance of the maximum and the minimum, using Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y] and their joint density.
3. Omitted-variable bias. In the model y = b1*x1 + b2*x2 + e, x2 is left out of the regression. Derive the limit of the estimated coefficient on x1, say when the bias is nonzero and what sign it has, and prove the result in matrix form.
4. Random graph. In a complete graph on n vertices with i.i.d. continuous random edge weights, a pair (a, b) is *good* if each endpoint is the other's maximum-weight neighbor. Compute the expected number of good pairs.
Notes
Indicator variables and linearity of expectation are the natural tools for any "expected number of" question.
Check each closed form on a small case (for example n = 2 or n = 3).

## Worked solution

### 1. Feasible common correlation

Assume all variables have finite positive variance, then standardize them. For n≥2 their correlation matrix is

$$R=(1-p)I_n+p\mathbf1\mathbf1^T.$$

The all-ones direction has eigenvalue 1+(n-1)p. Every perpendicular direction has eigenvalue 1-p. Covariance matrices are positive semidefinite, so both must be nonnegative:

$$\boxed{-1/(n-1)\le p\le1}.$$

This is sufficient too: take an n-dimensional standard Gaussian G and X=R^(1/2)G. It has covariance R, including singular endpoints. At p=1 all variables can be equal; at p=-1/(n-1) their sum has zero variance. For three variables the range is [-1/2,1]. At n=2 this becomes [-1,1], as expected. Requiring nonsingular joint covariance would make both inequalities strict.

### 2. Covariance of minimum and maximum

Let U=min Xi and V=max Xi for n≥2 independent Uniform(0,1) variables. To obtain U near u and V near v, choose ordered extreme indices in n(n-1) ways and put the remaining n-2 observations between them:

$$f_{U,V}(u,v)=n(n-1)(v-u)^{n-2},\qquad0<u<v<1.$$

The marginal extrema give E[U]=1/(n+1), E[V]=n/(n+1). For the mixed moment substitute u=vt in the inner integral:

$$\begin{aligned}
E[UV]&=n(n-1)\int_0^1\!\int_0^v uv(v-u)^{n-2}\,du\,dv\\
&=n(n-1)\int_0^1v^{n+1}\,dv\int_0^1t(1-t)^{n-2}\,dt\\
&=\frac{n(n-1)}{n+2}\frac1{n(n-1)}=\frac1{n+2}.
\end{aligned}$$

Therefore

$$\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)=\frac1{n+2}-\frac n{(n+1)^2}=\frac1{(n+1)^2(n+2)}}.$$

For n=2 it is 1/36. For n=1 the two extrema coincide, giving Var(X)=1/12; the final formula still agrees though the two-dimensional density does not apply.

### 3. Omitted-variable bias

Include an intercept, equivalently center x1,x2,y. Assume i.i.d. observations, finite second moments, Var(x1)>0 and E[x1 e]=0. Regressing y=b1 x1+b2 x2+e on x1 alone yields

$$\hat b_{short}=\frac{\sum x_{1i}y_i}{\sum x_{1i}^2}\xrightarrow{p} b_1+b_2\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(x_1,x_2)}{\operatorname{Var}(x_1)}.$$

Thus bias is nonzero exactly when b2 and the covariance are both nonzero under exogeneity; its sign is sign(b2 Cov(x1,x2)). Positive correlation alone does not determine the sign without b2. For example b2=3, Cov=2 and Var=4 give upward bias 1.5. If exogeneity fails, add Cov(x1,e)/Var(x1).

For centered design blocks X1 and X2, with X1 full column rank,

$$\hat\beta_{short}=(X_1^TX_1)^{-1}X_1^Ty
=\beta_1+(X_1^TX_1)^{-1}X_1^TX_2\beta_2+(X_1^TX_1)^{-1}X_1^Te.$$

If N^-1 X1^T X1→Q11 positive definite, N^-1 X1^T X2→Q12 and N^-1 X1^Te→0, the limit is β1+Q11^-1 Q12 β2. This also shows that a vector coefficient's bias can cancel even when individual omitted variables are correlated.

### 4. Mutual-maximum edges

For each unordered pair {a,b}, let Iab indicate that its edge is the largest at both endpoints. Its competitors are the union of incident edges: (n-1)+(n-1)-1=2n-3 distinct edges. With i.i.d. continuous weights ties have probability zero and each edge is equally likely to be their maximum. Hence

$$P(I_{ab}=1)=1/(2n-3),\qquad
\boxed{E[\#good]=\binom n2/(2n-3)}.$$

No independence between pair indicators is needed. At n=2 the sole edge is good, giving 1; at n=3 exactly the globally largest edge is good, also giving 1. Multiplying two independent 1/(n-1) probabilities is incorrect: the two endpoint events share the same edge. The linked checks enumerate all edge-order permutations for n=3,4 and verify these exact rational expectations; they do not substitute simulation for the derivation.
