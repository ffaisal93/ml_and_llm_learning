## Question

IBM intern and co-op follow-up interviews can be non-coding behavioural/resume rounds. One video-style competency screen gives one minute to prepare and three minutes to answer each question; another live interview focuses on motivation, missed deadlines, and project choices.

### Requirements

Video competency format:

Five questions.
One minute preparation per question.
Three minutes to answer each question.

Example prompts:

Tell us about a time a team project went very well. What part did you play, and how did you help the team succeed?
Think of a time when preparation paid off. What were you working on?

Live co-op / SDE interview prompts:

Biggest strength.
A time you missed a deadline.
Why IBM?
Why this role?
Resume project follow-ups: why the project, how you built it, and what technologies you used.
Notes
The live format may run 40-60 minutes with two interviewers and no coding.
Resume follow-ups are usually not deep architecture probes; they focus on motivation, ownership, and whether the listed stack is real.

## Worked solution

### Use the timed format deliberately

No resume, motivation or work history is supplied. The examples below are **fictional rehearsal material**, not the user's experience. In the one-minute preparation window, jot the situation, your specific action, one concrete result and what you learned. For a three-minute answer, spend roughly 30 seconds on context/task, 90 seconds on actions and decisions, 40 seconds on result and 20 seconds on reflection. Practice five separate questions under a timer; do not memorize one story that only loosely relates to every prompt.

### Teamwork and preparation examples

Fictional teamwork answer: “In a class data project, two teammates disagreed about whether to build a dashboard or improve the model. I asked each to list the grading requirements and the remaining work. I owned the data validation and wrote a shared interface so the model and dashboard could proceed in parallel. We agreed on a minimal end-to-end demo first, then added improvements only after it worked. We delivered the agreed demo and could explain each member's contribution. I learned that an explicit acceptance checklist resolved the disagreement better than arguing about which component was more impressive.” Replace this with an actual project and verifiable result; do not add percentages without records.

Fictional preparation answer: “Before a demo, I rehearsed on a fresh environment rather than my own laptop. The data path failed because a local file was assumed to exist. I documented setup, packaged a permitted small fixture and tested the demo offline. On presentation day we could run it from the clean setup. That preparation helped because it tested the same conditions the audience would see, and I now use a fresh-environment rehearsal before handoff.” Be ready to explain the file contract and exactly what you personally changed.

### Live follow-ups need direct answers

For biggest strength, choose a real repeatable behavior and support it with one example and a boundary, e.g. systematic debugging can be valuable but needs timeboxing. For a missed deadline, describe a genuine miss, when you recognized risk, who you informed, the scope/priority decision, outcome and subsequent process change. A fictional rehearsal: “I underestimated a data-cleaning dependency, told the lead once the risk became clear, delivered the required analysis first and renegotiated the optional visualization. I added an early data audit to later plans.” Do not convert a failure question into a disguised boast or blame teammates.

“Why IBM?” needs current, personally verified reasons: name a specific team/product/research area you have actually investigated, connect it to your demonstrated interests and explain what you hope to contribute/learn. No current company claims are supplied here, so do not invent a product initiative or say you have followed it for years. “Why this role?” connects its actual responsibilities to one real skill/project and one learning goal; the job description is a missing input required for a tailored final answer.

### Verify the resume story

For each listed project prepare a 60-second explanation: user/problem, why you chose it, your part, data/input-output, tools used and why, one hard bug/decision, validation and limitation. If Python/SQL/a framework appears on the resume, be able to describe a real use and trade-off; distinguish tutorial code from your own contribution. The live 40–60-minute round allows follow-ups, so keep a deeper example ready without turning a motivation answer into an unrequested architecture lecture. Record practice answers, check the three-minute limit and remove unsupported claims. Honest modest evidence is stronger than an impressive fabricated result.
