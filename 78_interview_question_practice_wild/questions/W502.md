## Question

Impact of Unexpected Rate Cuts on Asset Prices in Low Inflation

Given a surprise central bank rate cut in a low inflation environment, how will asset prices react to the news?

Example

### Input

```text
Current_InterestRate = 1.5%, New_InterestRate = 1.0%, InflationRate = 0.5%
```

## Worked solution

### Start with the discount-rate channel

A genuinely unexpected cut usually lowers the expected path of short-term rates. Holding future cash flows and risk premiums fixed, lower discount rates raise the present value of assets. In the example, the policy rate falls by 0.5 percentage points, or fifty basis points; low inflation of 0.5% does not itself fix future real rates or cash-flow expectations.

For an existing fixed-rate bond, lower relevant market yields imply a higher price. A first-order approximation is

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P}\approx-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\Delta y.$$

A bond with modified duration five would gain about 2.5% if its yield fell by the full fifty basis points, before convexity. The policy cut need not produce an equal move at every maturity; long yields depend on expectations and term premiums.

### Trace other assets and the information effect

Equities and property may rise through lower discount rates and financing costs. Long-duration growth cash flows are particularly sensitive in a discount-only calculation. Banks can face offsetting margin pressure. The domestic currency may depreciate if the expected interest differential falls, while gold may benefit from lower real opportunity cost, holding other forces fixed. Cash and newly issued short-term instruments generally offer lower future yields; an existing fixed-rate bond behaves differently from newly invested cash.

However, the surprise can reveal central-bank concern about a worse economy. If investors sharply cut expected profits, widen credit spreads or demand higher risk premiums, equities can fall even as safe government bonds rise. A currency can behave differently under safe-haven flows. Low inflation can signal room for easing or weak demand; the prompt does not tell us which dominates.

Thus the correct answer is conditional, not an exact price forecast: discount-rate effects support existing fixed cash-flow asset prices, while information about growth, inflation and risk can offset or reverse that support. To measure the immediate causal news reaction, examine a narrow announcement window and the unexpected change in rate expectations, rather than attributing a whole day's movement to the headline cut. The supplied policy/inflation numbers are insufficient to calculate specific stock, bond or exchange-rate prices.
