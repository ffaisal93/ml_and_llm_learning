## Question

Short-Run Stock Price Impact of Merger Announcement

Suppose A announces its merger with B. How will the stock prices of A and B change in the very short run?

Example

### Input

```text
Company_A_SharePrice = 100, Company_B_SharePrice = 50
```

## Worked solution

### Identify the buyer, target and payment terms

Assume A is the acquirer and B the target. The initial prices 100 and fifty do not determine announcement returns. We need the offer price or exchange ratio, the probability of completion, financing terms, standalone prospects and whether the deal was already anticipated.

For a cash offer of C per B share, a simple target-price approximation is

$$P_B\approx q\,\operatorname{PV}(C)+(1-q)P_{B,\mathrm{break}},$$

where q is completion probability and the break price is the expected value if the deal fails. For an illustrative sixty-dollar offer, 90% completion chance and fifty-dollar break value, ignoring discounting gives 59. The target rises toward the offer because holders expect a premium, but usually trades below it when completion risk/time matters. It could fall if the terms disappoint expectations or the announcement reveals bad standalone news.

### Explain the acquirer's ambiguous reaction

A's shareholders gain if their share of net synergy exceeds the premium, integration costs and financing/dilution effects. Overpayment or weak expected integration can make A fall; credible value creation can make it rise. “Acquirer always falls” is not a law. With stock consideration, the value offered to B moves with A's price and the exchange terms, producing linked price reactions. Fixed-value and fixed-share offers behave differently.

For the combined transaction, calculate market-value changes using shares outstanding, not by adding percentage returns or the two share prices. Abnormal returns should be compared with broad-market/sector movements over a suitable announcement window. Information leakage and anticipation reduce the portion of the deal value that appears exactly at announcement.

The concise interview answer is: a target often rises toward a risk-adjusted offer value; the acquirer may rise or fall depending on value retained after the premium and costs. The prompt's share prices alone cannot establish either sign or a numerical new price.
