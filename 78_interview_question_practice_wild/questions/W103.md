# W103. Explain a Regressing Production Model to a PM

**Priority group:** Second 100 — important

## Question

Capital One DS Power Day round 3, 45 minutes, role-play. The interviewer plays a non-technical Product Manager who asks why a model that performed well in validation is regressing in production. Explain the likely causes in plain English without technical jargon, and propose a diagnostic plan. The PM applies light pushback ("but we tested it before launch — what changed?") to test composure.

## Worked solution

### A plain-language conversation

“I would first check that we are comparing the same outcome, over the same amount of time, for similar customers. A drop could mean customers changed, information reached the model differently, or the way we count success changed. I don't yet know which explanation is correct. My first priority is to protect customers while we find out.”

PM: “But we tested it before launch — what changed?”

“The test showed how it worked on the customers and information in that test. Since launch, a new marketing campaign may have brought different customers, a field may have stopped arriving, or outcomes may still be incomplete. For example, if it takes a month to confirm a case, comparing this week's incomplete cases with last month's completed cases gives a misleading result. Testing reduces risk; it does not freeze the world.”

### A diagnostic plan with owners and decisions

In the first hour, have the product analyst verify the metric definition, dates, customer count and completed outcome window. Compare the same customer groups before and after launch, show the range of uncertainty, and count the affected decisions. Have the on-call engineer check the release time, missing information, delays and changes to the cutoff used to trigger actions. Replay identical saved examples through the test and live systems; different answers indicate a deployment problem.

By the end of the day, compare returning and new customers, channels, regions and product types. A worse overall result with unchanged results within each group suggests a change in customer mix; worsening within the same group needs deeper investigation. Have the data owner inspect a small sample of affected cases and compare them with successful cases, including cases the model never acted on. Check whether actual customer behavior changed or whether the recorded outcomes are wrong.

For example, if the model used to identify 800 of 1,000 confirmed cases and now identifies 700, that means 100 additional misses per 1,000 cases. Before calling this a model failure, ensure both periods contain fully confirmed outcomes and comparable customers. Then estimate the customer and business cost with the PM; do not invent that cost from an accuracy number.

### Contain, repair and report

“If we see customer harm above our agreed limit, I recommend restoring the last safe release or routing the affected decisions to the existing review process today. If the issue is delayed information, we fix that feed; if customer behavior changed, we test an updated model against recent completed cases before releasing it gradually. Retraining is one possible repair, not the diagnosis.”

Agree on an owner and update time for every check. Release the repair first to a small monitored group, with the old process as a comparison; require recovery of the affected metric and no material deterioration in critical customer groups. Report confirmed facts, remaining explanations, containment status and the next decision in the same short format. If the PM asks for certainty prematurely, say what evidence would distinguish the remaining causes and when it can arrive.
