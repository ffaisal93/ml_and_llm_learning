## Worked solution

### The fit is a projection of noise

The no-intercept fitted vector is $P_xy$, where $P_x=xx^T/(x^Tx)$. Since $P_x$ is an orthogonal projector,

$$R^2_{in}=\frac{(x^Ty)^2}{(x^Tx)(y^Ty)}=\cos^2\theta.$$

Condition on any nonzero x and rotate coordinates so its direction is the first axis. The standard Gaussian y remains isotropic. Its squared component along x is $U\sim\chi^2_1$, and perpendicular energy is $W\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$, independent. Therefore

$$R^2_{in}=\frac{U}{U+W}\sim\operatorname{Beta}\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right),\qquad E[R^2_{in}]=1/n.$$

It is $O_p(1/n)$ and converges to zero in probability; more precisely, $nR^2_{in}\Rightarrow\chi^2_1$. Under a nested sequence of i.i.d. observations, the strong law gives almost-sure convergence to zero as well. It is not identically zero at finite n: OLS chooses a direction/scale after seeing y. Orthogonal x,y give zero, while a nonzero multiple gives one, matching both checks.

With an intercept, center both vectors. The residual subspace has dimension n-1; the centered correlation squared has distribution $\operatorname{Beta}(1/2,(n-2)/2)$ and expectation $1/(n-1)$. The intercept itself consumes one degree of freedom. This is a different denominator/statistic from the uncentered no-intercept definition.

### Fresh-sample fit need not beat predicting zero

For the independent fixed-size test set, expand the definition:

$$R^2_{test}=\frac{2\hat\beta x_t^Ty_t-\hat\beta^2\|x_t\|^2}{\|y_t\|^2}.$$

The denominator is positive almost surely, even for m=1. A wrong-signed prediction or excessive coefficient can make the numerator negative; zero is the true conditional-mean predictor. Since $\hat\beta\to0$ in probability as training n grows, $R^2_{test}\to0$ in probability for every fixed m, without needing m to grow. If the same test sample is fixed across a nested training sequence, the limit is almost sure. Conditional on $\hat\beta$, test MSE has expectation $1+\hat\beta^2$, which is worse than the oracle value 1 unless the fitted coefficient is zero. For n>2, $E[\hat\beta^2]=1/(n-2)$. Do not infer the expectation of the ratio $R^2_{test}$ by dividing expected sums; for m<=2 inverse test energy has divergent moments. Geometric finite-sample fit and predictive usefulness are separate claims.
