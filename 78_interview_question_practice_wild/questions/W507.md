## Question

A wheat farm has ripe crops in three fields and one harvesting crew. You advise the owner on three decisions in turn: the order in which to harvest the fields, when to harvest a fourth field whose value is still growing, and whether to sell the harvested grain now or store it. Each decision needs a rule you can justify, a number, and the one assumption that would change your answer.

### Case data

Decision 1: harvest order. The crew harvests one field at a time, starting on day 0. While a field stands ripe and unharvested, it loses 1% of its crop value per day. A field's loss is counted up to the day its harvest finishes.

field	days to harvest	crop value
A	4	USD 240k
B	2	USD 210k
C	3	USD 90k

Decision 2: harvest timing. Field D is worth USD 100k if harvested today (day 0). Each day of waiting adds USD 2k of value, because the grain dries in the field. A hailstorm may arrive; if it hits before the harvest, half of the crop is lost. The probability that the storm has hit before a harvest on day t is:

harvest day t	0	1	2	3	4	5
storm already hit	0%	1%	3%	7%	14%	28%

Decision 3: sell now or store. After the harvest the farm holds 50,000 bushels. The local spot price today is USD 5.80 per bushel. A buyer offers a forward contract at USD 6.25 per bushel for delivery in 3 months. Storage costs USD 0.06 per bushel per month. Money tied up in grain has an opportunity cost of 6% a year, simple interest.

What to answer
1. Which harvest order minimizes the total loss across fields A, B and C, and what is that loss? Give the rule that picks the order and explain why it is optimal.
2. On which day should field D be harvested to maximize its expected value, and what is that expected value? State the marginal rule behind the choice.
3. Should the farm sell now or store and sell forward? Give the net price per bushel of each option and the total difference.
4. Sensitivity. At what monthly storage cost would the owner be indifferent in Decision 3? Which single input, if it moved a little, would change one of your three recommendations?
Assumptions
All three fields in Decision 1 are ripe on day 0 and the crew works without breaks.
Field D in Decision 2 is harvested by a separate contract crew, so its timing does not depend on the Decision 1 schedule.
Use simple interest for the opportunity cost in Decision 3.
Ignore harvesting costs; they are the same in every option.
In Decision 3, the forward contract carries no default risk and the grain does not spoil in storage.

### Constraints

45 minutes. Spend the first minutes on the structure, then compute each decision with the given numbers.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

1. Under the linear 1%-per-day loss convention, minimize sum_i w_i*C_i with w_i=0.01*value_i. A pairwise interchange argument orders jobs by descending w_i/processing_days: B (1050/day²), A (600), C (300). Completion days 2,6,9 give loss 0.01*(210000*2+240000*6+90000*9)=26700.
2. For D, expected value at day t is (100000+2000*t)*(1-0.5*storm_probability_t). Across days 0–5 this gives [100000,101490,102440,102290,100440,94600], so choose day 2 under the supplied table. Wait only while the next day’s expected gain is positive.
3. For storage, subtract 3*0.06=0.18 per bushel and simple opportunity cost 5.80*0.06*(3/12)=0.087 from the forward price. Net forward value is 5.983 versus immediate 5.80, difference 0.183*50000=9150.
4. Indifference monthly storage cost is (6.25-5.80-0.087)/3=0.121 per bushel. Day 3 would beat day 2 if its cumulative hail probability dropped below about 6.717%, showing a sensitive boundary. Compounding crop losses or different financing/forward risks would require a revised model.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Write the units and driver equation before the conclusion

Separate arithmetic implied by the supplied numbers from empirical facts or predictions the prompt does not establish. A business decomposition might be

$$
\Pi=V\,(p+s-c_v)-C_f,
$$

where $V$ is visits/customers, $p$ price, $s$ other per-unit revenue, $c_v$ variable cost and $C_f$ fixed cost. State whether a “10% drop” is relative or a ten-percentage-point change in utilization; those give different $V$ values.

For valuation ratios, earnings equal market capitalization divided by a compatible P/E, but EPS additionally requires the share count. Combined earnings are not combined EPS. For forecast reactions, a lower discount rate and worse expected cash flows can push values in opposite directions; the headline alone need not identify a numerical outcome.

For estimation, propagate uncertain input ranges rather than presenting assumptions as measured current data. For a toy wager, expected payoff and outcome risk are different; no incomplete odds table determines an appropriate real stake.

Finish with a sensitivity: change the most influential uncertain input and locate the decision boundary. Explain the assumption that could reverse the answer. These are educational calculations and conditional reasoning, not a recommendation to invest, gamble or execute a transaction.

#### Put the result in mathematical notation

Under linear daily losses, minimize $\sum_i w_i C_i$ by descending $w_i/p_i$, with $w_i=0.01V_i$. The expected waiting-field value is
$$\mathbb E[V_D(t)]=(100000+2000t)\left(1-\frac{P(\text{hail by }t)}2\right).$$
For storage under the supplied simple-interest assumptions,
$$P_{\rm net}=6.25-3(0.06)-5.80(0.06)(3/12)=5.983.$$
Units matter: the storage rate is per bushel per month, while the opportunity rate is annual. Changing those conventions changes the arithmetic.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Using largest crop value alone to order fields or forgetting the opportunity cost when comparing stored grain with cash today.
