## Question

A wheat farm has ripe crops in three fields and one harvesting crew. You advise the owner on three decisions in turn: the order in which to harvest the fields, when to harvest a fourth field whose value is still growing, and whether to sell the harvested grain now or store it. Each decision needs a rule you can justify, a number, and the one assumption that would change your answer.

### Case data

Decision 1: harvest order. The crew harvests one field at a time, starting on day 0. While a field stands ripe and unharvested, it loses 1% of its crop value per day. A field's loss is counted up to the day its harvest finishes.

field	days to harvest	crop value
A	4	USD 240k
B	2	USD 210k
C	3	USD 90k

Decision 2: harvest timing. Field D is worth USD 100k if harvested today (day 0). Each day of waiting adds USD 2k of value, because the grain dries in the field. A hailstorm may arrive; if it hits before the harvest, half of the crop is lost. The probability that the storm has hit before a harvest on day t is:

harvest day t	0	1	2	3	4	5
storm already hit	0%	1%	3%	7%	14%	28%

Decision 3: sell now or store. After the harvest the farm holds 50,000 bushels. The local spot price today is USD 5.80 per bushel. A buyer offers a forward contract at USD 6.25 per bushel for delivery in 3 months. Storage costs USD 0.06 per bushel per month. Money tied up in grain has an opportunity cost of 6% a year, simple interest.

What to answer
1. Which harvest order minimizes the total loss across fields A, B and C, and what is that loss? Give the rule that picks the order and explain why it is optimal.
2. On which day should field D be harvested to maximize its expected value, and what is that expected value? State the marginal rule behind the choice.
3. Should the farm sell now or store and sell forward? Give the net price per bushel of each option and the total difference.
4. Sensitivity. At what monthly storage cost would the owner be indifferent in Decision 3? Which single input, if it moved a little, would change one of your three recommendations?
Assumptions
All three fields in Decision 1 are ripe on day 0 and the crew works without breaks.
Field D in Decision 2 is harvested by a separate contract crew, so its timing does not depend on the Decision 1 schedule.
Use simple interest for the opportunity cost in Decision 3.
Ignore harvesting costs; they are the same in every option.
In Decision 3, the forward contract carries no default risk and the grain does not spoil in storage.

### Constraints

45 minutes. Spend the first minutes on the structure, then compute each decision with the given numbers.

## Worked solution

### 1. Harvest valuable waiting time first

Interpret the stated one-percent daily loss as **linear** loss of the original field value. If field i has processing time $p_i$ and daily loss weight $w_i=0.01V_i$, minimize $\sum_i w_iC_i$, where $C_i$ is completion day. Comparing adjacent orders i→j and j→i gives a difference $w_jp_i-w_ip_j$, so i should go first when $p_i/w_i\le p_j/w_j$. This is equivalent to descending value per harvest day.

A has value/day sixty thousand, B 105 thousand and C thirty thousand. Order **B, A, C**, finishing on days 2, 6 and 9. Loss is

$$0.01(210{,}000\cdot2+240{,}000\cdot6+90{,}000\cdot9)=26{,}700.$$

The field's own harvest duration counts because loss continues until completion. The adjacent-swap argument proves optimality under the stated linear, all-ready, nonpreemptive assumptions. If daily loss means compounding or the crew can partially save a field during harvesting, recompute the objective rather than blindly using this rule.

### 2. Choose D's day by expected value

With value $V(t)=100+2t$ thousand and cumulative storm probability $q_t$, expected value is $V(t)(1-q_t/2)$. On days zero through five the values, in thousands, are

| Day | Expected value |
|---|---:|
| 0 | 100.00 |
| 1 | 101.49 |
| 2 | 102.44 |
| 3 | 102.29 |
| 4 | 100.44 |
| 5 | 94.60 |

Harvest on **day two**, with expected value 102,440. The marginal rule is to wait another day only if the added growth, adjusted for storm risk, exceeds the increase in expected storm loss. Comparing the actual discrete alternatives is necessary; the small decline from day two to three determines the choice. This assumes the storm halves the modeled harvest value and no further storm damage beyond that half is counted.

### 3. Compare storage at a common economic basis

Immediate sale gives 5.80 per bushel. Three months' storage costs 0.18, and the opportunity cost of holding grain rather than selling now is $5.80\times0.06\times3/12=0.087$. The forward option's net, after those stated costs, is

$$6.25-0.18-0.087=5.983.$$

That is 0.183 more per bushel, or **9,150** on 50,000 bushels. Store and lock the forward sale under the no-default/no-spoilage assumptions. These are simple-interest carry calculations; if comparing exact present values instead, use a consistent discounting convention for every cash flow and storage-payment date.

### 4. Sensitivity

With monthly storage cost s, indifference satisfies $6.25-3s-0.087=5.80$, giving **s=0.121 per bushel per month**. The forward price break-even is 6.067 at the stated storage cost. A particularly sensitive input is day-three cumulative storm probability: day three beats day two when $106(1-q_3/2)>102.44$, or $q_3<6.717\%$. The supplied seven percent is close, so a modest forecast revision can change the harvest day. Crew availability changes ordering; counterparty/default or spoilage risk can change storage economics.
