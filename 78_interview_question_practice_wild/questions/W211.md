## Question

Present one significant project from your own experience as a clear, structured deep dive, as if you were walking a technical audience through it.

What to answer
1. Context: what was the business goal, how large was the team, and what was your role?
2. Problem and constraints: what made it hard or ambiguous?
3. Your contribution: which specific part did you own, and where did you make the decisions?
4. Key decision: name one fork where you chose option A over B, the alternatives you considered, and why.
5. Outcome: which measurable results did it produce, and how did they tie back to the goal?
6. Retrospective: what would you do differently, and what did you learn?
Expectations
Choose a project you can defend in detail, including anything written on your resume about it.
The primary narrative fits in about 20 minutes, leaving room for follow-up questions on any step.
Say "I" for your own decisions and "we" for the team's; be specific about scope (people, quarters, scale).

## Worked solution

### A twenty-minute project narrative

The question asks for your experience, which is not provided. This is an **illustrative example** to adapt truthfully; the figures below are assumed rehearsal figures, not measured achievements.

**Context (2 minutes).** “A four-engineer team was replacing a nightly customer-status report with a near-real-time page. I owned the event-consumer protocol and rollout over one quarter; product owned status definitions and another engineer owned the UI. Users were opening support tickets because the old page could be a day stale.”

**Problem and constraints (3 minutes).** Explain that three upstream systems could send duplicates or deliver events out of order, the source database could not sustain per-page fan-out, and a wrong status was worse than an explicit stale warning. Define the invariant: a resource's visible version never goes backwards, and every accepted source update eventually becomes visible or produces an actionable reconciliation error.

**Contribution (4 minutes).** “I implemented a transactional outbox in the owning service, idempotent consumers keyed by event ID, version checks, a reconciliation task and a canary plan. I did not build the queue platform.” Walk a concrete case: version 8 arrives before version 7; persist 8, acknowledge 7 without overwriting it. A missing version triggers source reconciliation rather than unbounded retry.

**Decision (4 minutes).** Choose an asynchronously maintained read model over synchronous fan-out. It meets read latency and isolates source outages, at the cost of freshness lag and a second representation to operate. Show how the stale indicator and lag alert make this trade-off visible. A synchronous join remains preferable for low traffic requiring the latest value at every read.

**Outcome (3 minutes).** The rehearsal target might be 99% of changes visible within one minute, p95 page read under 200 ms, and fewer status-related tickets. In your real answer, report observed baseline and post-launch windows, request volumes and caveats such as simultaneous UI changes. If only correctness drills and user feedback exist, say so; targets are not outcomes.

**Retrospective (4 minutes).** “I initially underestimated upstream schema changes. I would agree versioned contracts and ownership before implementation, and test deletes and delayed events earlier.” Explain what changed in the next project. Keep the same project throughout and distinguish “I decided/implemented” from “we agreed/shipped.” The strongest follow-up is a real event trace, rollout incident or result table that you can explain without notes.
