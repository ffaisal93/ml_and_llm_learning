## Question

Market-Making Interval Estimation Game

A QR/QT interview game: across five rounds the interviewer asks a numeric estimation question and you respond with a closed interval [L, U]. You score 0 if the true answer falls outside your interval and L/U if it falls inside — so tighter honest intervals score higher. The target is reaching at least 2.0 total points.

### Requirements

Five rounds; the goal is to accumulate at least 2.0 total points.
Each round: the interviewer poses a numeric question and you report a closed interval [L, U] you believe contains the answer.
Scoring: if the true answer is outside [L, U], you score 0 for that round; if inside, you score L/U. A wider interval is safer but scores less; a tighter interval scores more but risks missing.

### Examples

True answer = 16: reporting [20, 40] scores 0 (16 is outside); reporting [10, 30] scores 10/30 ≈ 0.33.
Notes
The game directly tests calibrated estimation: you must balance the probability of containment against the L/U reward, which pushes you to the tightest interval you still trust.
It is typically preceded by the classic HR "eight big questions" round (English/communication screen) and followed by a structured-problem (SPD) round.

## Worked solution

### Optimize expected score, not interval width alone

Assume the answer is positive, so $0<L\le U$ and $L/U$ is a meaningful reward. For a subjective distribution with CDF F, expected score is

$$E[S(L,U)]=\frac LU\,P(L\le X\le U).$$

A very broad interval has high containment but a small width reward; a point interval scores almost surely zero for a continuous unknown answer. The best interval depends on your uncertainty distribution, not a universal confidence level. For the example, `[20,40]` misses sixteen and scores zero; `[10,30]` contains it and scores one third.

### Build an interval from an estimate

Break an unfamiliar quantity into measurable factors. For an attendance estimate, use population × eligible fraction × participation fraction, with plausible ranges for each. State units and order-of-magnitude checks. Work in log space when errors are multiplicative: an interval $[\hat x/c,c\hat x]$ has reward $1/c^2$ if it hits. Estimate the chance of containing X for several c values and compare expected scores. A 70%-coverage interval with L/U=0.7 has expected score 0.49; a 95%-coverage interval with ratio 0.3 has expected score 0.285.

Intervals need not be symmetric around a point estimate. If uncertainty is skewed or has a hard bound, shift the interval accordingly. With a discrete set of plausible values, enumerate reasonable endpoint pairs and evaluate containment mass times L/U.

### Five-round strategy and target

To average two total points across five similar rounds, mean expected score must be at least 0.4 per round, but expectation does not guarantee reaching the target. The probability of reaching two depends on the distribution of each reward and dependence among questions. If the objective is specifically that probability, the best final-round choice may differ depending on accumulated score. For instance, with 1.6 points before the last round, an interval whose successful reward is below 0.4 cannot reach the target regardless of its coverage.

Track actual score, explain confidence honestly and update uncertainty from feedback. Do not repeatedly choose narrow intervals based on confidence unsupported by decomposition or facts. For zero/negative answers the stated L/U rule can be undefined or pathological; ask the interviewer to define the allowed domain/scoring convention instead of silently applying a positive-number strategy.
