3. Implement ACE

ACE measures how well the confidence of the model matches its accuracy.
1. The confidence of an example is exp(logprob), a number in (0, 1].
2. Split [0, 1] into m_bins equal-width bins: bin m covers [m/m_bins, (m+1)/m_bins). A confidence of exactly 1.0 belongs to the last bin.
3. For every non-empty bin B, acc(B) is the mean accuracy and conf(B) the mean confidence of the examples in it.
4. ACE = mean over the non-empty bins of |acc(B) - conf(B)|. Empty bins are skipped: they are not counted as zero and they are not in the denominator.
5. With no examples, return 0.0.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

#### Why can cross-entropy worsen while accuracy stays unchanged?

Accuracy uses the chosen class; cross-entropy also measures how much probability the model assigns to the true class.

1. Separate correct and incorrect predictions and inspect confidence changes.
2. For a true-class probability p, loss is -log(p), which grows without bound as p approaches zero.
3. Track validation loss, accuracy and calibration together; consider early stopping or calibration using a separate validation set.

#### Bound cross-entropy when a prediction is correct or incorrect.

The label probability is constrained by whether it is the largest class probability, not merely by the number of classes.

1. For C classes and a correct argmax, p_true>=1/C, so $0\le\ell\le\log C$, subject to tie conventions.
2. For an incorrect argmax, p_true<=1/2 and $\ell\ge\log2$, with the lower bound an infimum when strict inequality is required.
3. There is no finite upper bound for an incorrect prediction as p_true approaches zero. For N examples with k errors, the mean-loss lower bound is (k/N)*log(2). If k=0 the upper bound is log(C); if k>0 there is no finite upper bound.

#### Calculate average calibration error from confidence bins.

Calibration compares predicted confidence with observed correctness; it is different from classification accuracy.

1. Convert predicted-class log probabilities to confidence with exp(logprob), then assign equal-width bins in [0,1].
2. Put confidence exactly 1 in the last bin. For each non-empty bin compute abs(mean_accuracy-mean_confidence).
3. For this ACE definition, average gaps equally across non-empty bins and return zero for no examples. A sample-weighted version is a different metric.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Specify which calibration error you mean

Confidence here is the probability of the **predicted** class, not necessarily the true class. If its stored value is log confidence $\ell_i$, recover $c_i=e^{\ell_i}$ and let $a_i\in\{0,1\}$ indicate correctness.

Split $[0,1]$ into $M$ equal-width bins, placing exactly $1$ in the final bin. For nonempty bins $\mathcal B_+$, this exercise defines

$$
\operatorname{ACE}=\frac1{|\mathcal B_+|}\sum_{b\in\mathcal B_+}
\left|\frac{\sum_{i\in b}a_i}{n_b}-\frac{\sum_{i\in b}c_i}{n_b}\right|.
$$

Every nonempty bin receives equal weight. A population-weighted ECE instead weights each gap by $n_b/N$ and is a different statistic. Empty bins are omitted rather than counted as zero-error bins.

For confidence values $[0.9,0.95,1,0.5,0.55]$ and correctness $[1,1,1,0,1]$ at five bins, the first group has accuracy $1$ and mean confidence $0.95$, gap $0.05$; the second has accuracy $0.5$ and confidence $0.525$, gap $0.025$. ACE is $(0.05+0.025)/2=0.0375$.

Show bin counts alongside the number. Small bins and examples crossing a boundary can make the curve spiky even without a large change in the model. Use independent calibration/evaluation data and inspect proper scores such as log loss too; one binned metric is not a complete account of probability quality.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**Bound cross-entropy when a prediction is correct or incorrect.** For 10 classes, a correct prediction's loss cannot exceed log(10) under an ordinary argmax decision; an incorrect prediction can have arbitrarily large loss.

With k>0 errors in N examples, is mean loss bounded above? No; even one error can have unbounded loss.

**Calculate average calibration error from confidence bins.** Bins with gaps 0.05 and 0.025 give ACE=0.0375 regardless of their different populations under the equal-bin convention.

Why can ACE be noisy? Small changes can move examples across bin boundaries; inspect bin counts and reliability plots.
