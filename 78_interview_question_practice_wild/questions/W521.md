## Question

Answer a conversational culture and values question set about why you want to work at a mission-driven travel and hospitality marketplace, and what you care about beyond the job description.

What to answer
1. Why this company specifically? Name one concrete experience or product observation that connects you to its mission of belonging.
2. What do you think is the biggest problem in the world, and what are you doing about it?
3. Describe a travel experience (or time in an unfamiliar place) that changed how you think.
4. Tell about a time you were both a host and a guest, or gave and received hard feedback. What did each side teach you?
5. Describe a decision at a previous job where your values shaped the choice, including what it cost you.
6. Name a mentor or a risk you took, and how it shaped how you work today.
Expectations
Answers are specific and biographical: real names of places, projects and people (anonymised if needed), not slogans.
Each answer is 1 to 2 minutes spoken.
Answers are your own; do not invent experiences.

## Worked solution

### Prepare six distinct true stories

This question explicitly requires your biography. Your travel history, employer decisions and mentors are not provided, so a completed first-person account would be invented. Use the response structures below to prepare six spoken answers, each one to two minutes. Replace brackets with verifiable details; if you have no relevant travel story, use an unfamiliar local/community setting and say so.

**1. Why this company?** Start with one product interaction or observation, connect it to belonging, then to the actual role. A usable structure is: “When [real experience], I noticed [specific barrier or helpful feature]. That made belonging concrete for me because [effect on a person]. I want to work on [team problem] where my [demonstrated skill] could improve [outcome].” Avoid claiming every marketplace interaction is inclusive; a thoughtful limitation makes the motivation more credible.

**2. Biggest problem and action.** Choose a problem you genuinely care about, explain its human impact and name your own sustained action. Scope the claim: “One problem I care deeply about is [problem], especially [specific population]. I contribute through [actual action/frequency], and learned [lesson]. I cannot solve the whole problem, but my next step is [realistic commitment].” Do not invent volunteering or donations. A modest consistent contribution is stronger than a heroic hypothetical.

**3. Unfamiliar-place experience.** Describe the place/context, your initial assumption, the interaction that challenged it and a lasting behavior change. The insight should be more specific than “people are different.” Explain how you now listen, ask for context or avoid treating your own norm as universal.

**4. Host/guest or hard feedback.** Tell both sides. As a host or feedback giver, what did you do to make expectations clear without humiliating someone? As a guest or receiver, what surprised you and what did you change? Include the other person's perspective instead of portraying yourself as the sole teacher.

**5. A costly values decision.** Identify the competing legitimate goals, your responsibility, alternatives and the actual cost of your choice—time, scope, convenience or an uncomfortable conversation. Explain the result and what you would improve. “I value integrity” without a decision is not evidence; neither is a story in which doing the right thing had no trade-off.

**6. Mentor or risk.** Name or anonymize a real person/decision, describe the specific advice or uncertainty and show a later behavior it changed. Distinguish the result from the lesson: a sensible risk can fail, and a lucky success does not prove sound reasoning.

### Rehearse for follow-up depth

Write three facts for each story: what you personally did, what someone else did and what evidence supports the result. Speak a ninety-second version, then practice “What did that cost?”, “What would the other person say?” and “What changed afterward?” Keep personal details within your comfort and confidentiality boundaries. The goal is a coherent truthful account, not memorizing six polished fictional speeches.
