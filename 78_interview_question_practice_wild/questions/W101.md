## Question

Explain how bias and variance describe the behavior of a classifier across different training samples. Use probability prediction and hard-label prediction to show why the familiar squared-loss decomposition does not transfer unchanged to 0-1 loss.

### Requirements

1. At a fixed input x, define the bias and variance of a learned probability p_D(x) over random training sets D.
2. Derive the expected squared-loss decomposition for a fresh binary label, including irreducible noise. State the independence assumption that makes the cross terms vanish.
3. For binary 0-1 loss, define the Bayes label, the main prediction, bias, variance and net variance. Explain why variance can reduce error when the main prediction is biased.
4. Use k-nearest neighbors as a concrete example. Compare small and large k, then explain how model complexity, regularization and training-set size usually affect bias and variance.
5. Distinguish reducible estimation error from label noise, and note the limits of these pointwise statements.
Deliverable

Give a mathematical explanation followed by practical guidance for diagnosing underfitting and overfitting in classification.

## Worked solution

### Probability estimates and squared loss

Fix $x$ and let $\eta=P(Y=1\mid x)$, $\mu=E_D[p_D(x)]$. The probability bias is $\mu-\eta$ and the variance is $E_D[(p_D-\mu)^2]$. For a fresh $Y\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\eta)$ independent of training set $D$ conditional on $x$,

$$E_{D,Y}[(Y-p_D)^2\mid x]=\eta(1-\eta)+(\mu-\eta)^2+\operatorname{Var}_D(p_D).$$

Expand $Y-p_D=(Y-\eta)+(\eta-\mu)+(\mu-p_D)$. The first and last terms have zero mean; their cross product has zero expectation by conditional independence. The three surviving terms are label noise, squared bias, and variance. If the test label was reused during training, that independence argument fails.

### Hard labels require a different decomposition

Let $h_D=1[p_D\ge .5]$, Bayes label $y^*=1[\eta\ge .5]$, and main prediction $m=\operatorname{mode}_D h_D$, using a fixed tie rule. Define $N=\min(\eta,1-\eta)$, $B=1[m\ne y^*]$ and $V=P_D(h_D\ne m)$. Then

$$E_D P(Y\ne h_D\mid D,x)=N+(1-2N)\{B+(1-2B)V\}.$$

Indeed, a prediction equal to $y^*$ has error $N$, while the other label has error $1-N$. Its probability of differing from Bayes is $V$ when $B=0$, and $1-V$ when $B=1$. Define **net variance** $V_{net}=(1-2B)V$; its error contribution is $(1-2N)V_{net}$. This convention distinguishes net variance from the unweighted disagreement $V$. Some texts absorb $(1-2N)$ into the term called net variance.

For example, $\eta=.8$ and a biased main label $m=0$ with $V=.2$ gives error $.2+.6(1-.2)=.68$, below the $.8$ error of always predicting 0. Variation sometimes flips the wrong main label to the right one. For an unbiased main label the same variance instead raises error to $.32$. Squared-loss variance is always nonnegative; the hard-label contribution need not be. At $\eta=.5$, all hard labels have error $.5$.

### Diagnose with evidence

With k-NN, small $k$ follows local sample fluctuations: usually low smoothing bias and high variance. Larger $k$ averages labels and reduces variance but can smooth across a decision boundary and introduce bias; $k=n$ predicts global prevalence. At a clean point, averaging independent neighboring Bernoulli labels has variance approximately $\eta(1-\eta)/k$, with additional bias from nonlocal neighbors.

Increasing capacity or weakening regularization often reduces approximation bias and increases sampling variance; more independent data often reduces estimation variance. These are tendencies, not monotonic laws for all classifiers. In consistent k-NN asymptotics, $k\to\infty$ and $k/n\to0$ allow both averaging and shrinking neighborhoods.

High training and validation error warrants checks for bad features, optimization failure and excessive constraints before increasing capacity. Low training error with poor validation warrants leakage-free learning curves, repeated/grouped splits, and controlled regularization or data-size changes. Compare probability losses and calibration as well as accuracy: a threshold can hide probability improvements. Reducible estimation error comes from finite data and fitting; irreducible label noise remains even at $p_D=\eta$. Model misspecification is a separate reducible approximation limitation. These are pointwise, distribution-dependent statements; average over deployment $x$, and examine class and subgroup errors before drawing operational conclusions.
