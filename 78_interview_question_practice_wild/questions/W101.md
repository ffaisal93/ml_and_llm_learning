## Question

<pre class="question-text" style="white-space:pre-wrap;font-family:inherit;line-height:1.55">Explain how bias and variance describe the behavior of a classifier across different training samples. Use probability prediction and hard-label prediction to show why the familiar squared-loss decomposition does not transfer unchanged to 0-1 loss.

Requirements
1.
At a fixed input x, define the bias and variance of a learned probability p_D(x) over random training sets D.
2.
Derive the expected squared-loss decomposition for a fresh binary label, including irreducible noise. State the independence assumption that makes the cross terms vanish.
3.
For binary 0-1 loss, define the Bayes label, the main prediction, bias, variance and net variance. Explain why variance can reduce error when the main prediction is biased.
4.
Use k-nearest neighbors as a concrete example. Compare small and large k, then explain how model complexity, regularization and training-set size usually affect bias and variance.
5.
Distinguish reducible estimation error from label noise, and note the limits of these pointwise statements.
Deliverable

Give a mathematical explanation followed by practical guidance for diagnosing underfitting and overfitting in classification.</pre>

## Worked solution

1. Diagnose high bias versus high variance. Use training and validation behavior to distinguish insufficient fitting from poor generalization.
2. If both errors remain high, check the pipeline and optimization first, then consider inadequate features or capacity.
3. If training error is low but validation error is high, check leakage-free splitting, distribution shift and overfitting.
4. Use learning curves and controlled changes: more data, regularization, simpler models or better features depending on the diagnosis.

### Small example / sanity check

Training error 1% and validation error 25% suggests a generalization problem, but a group-distribution mismatch could be the cause rather than model complexity alone.

### Common trap

Treating the bias–variance story as a diagnosis without inspecting label noise, split structure or optimization failure.
