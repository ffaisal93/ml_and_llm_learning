## Question

L6 behavioral conducted by a senior Director. Discussion-style — interviewer probes past leadership stories (tree-level and forest-level) and weaves in policy-vs-efficiency dilemmas that feel like trolley-problem variants. No standard 'tell me about a time' prompts; expect to defend judgments live.

### Requirements

60-minute discussion-style behavioral, not a checklist of standard prompts.
Cover past projects at two levels: project-detail ('the tree') and strategic / cross-team ('the forest').
Be ready for policy-vs-efficiency dilemmas presented as hypotheticals — e.g. 'a new safety policy slows down the simulator pipeline; how do you weigh it against shipping velocity?'
The interviewer is typically a senior D / VP-level engineer; expect direct push-back and follow-up on every claim.
Notes
Structure stories with cause → choice → outcome → reflection. Senior interviewers credit the reflection layer specifically — what would you do differently, what did the experience change in your decision framework.
Tree vs forest. Have two of each kind of story prepared:
Tree-level: concrete engineering decision, with numbers, that you owned end-to-end.
Forest-level: cross-team or organizational decision where you influenced direction without holding the authority.
Policy-vs-efficiency dilemmas. Frame the response around explicit trade-off axes (safety, velocity, cost, fairness, downstream maintenance). Pick a stance and defend it; refusing to commit is a documented red flag at the L6 bar.
Influence without authority. Be ready to describe a specific time you changed another team's roadmap. The interviewer will ask 'what would have happened if you hadn't pushed' — have a credible counterfactual.
Pushback handling. When the interviewer challenges a choice, restate the trade-off in their language before defending. This signals you can update on new information rather than reciting a memorized story.
Don't oversell. Senior interviewers notice puffed-up impact claims; quantify outcomes conservatively and acknowledge collaborators by name.
Specific Waymo themes that have surfaced: decisions weighing rider safety against fleet utilization; ML model promotion gates; managing cross-team review for safety-critical changes; deprecating a beloved feature for compliance reasons.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

Distinguish a mandatory constraint from a process that can be improved through an authorized change.

1. Clarify the purpose, owner and actual requirements of the policy.
2. Find compliant alternatives or propose a reviewed exception with evidence about benefits, risks and controls.
3. Document the decision and measure the result; do not frame secretly bypassing a requirement as good ownership.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Give evidence for a decision, not a polished but untestable story

Use an actual experience and separate your responsibility from the team's. State the problem and constraint, the alternative you considered, your action and the observable result. If you do not have a defensible metric, say what evidence you do have instead of inventing a percentage.

For a technical project, explain the data/split, baseline, hardest failure and the experiment that changed your mind. For conflict or feedback, represent the other person's concern fairly and show how you tested assumptions or changed behavior. “I won the argument” is not the project outcome.

For a research claim, distinguish mechanism from evidence. A bigger training dataset or compute budget can confound an architecture comparison. Ask which ablation separates the proposed explanation from a plausible alternative and whether uncertainty is large relative to the claimed gain.

A useful spoken structure is problem → constraint → decision → evidence → limitation → next step. Keep a longer derivation or failure trace ready for follow-up rather than hiding weak reasoning behind jargon. A motivation/timeline question needs a direct truthful answer, not a generic success story; protect confidential details and never fabricate offers or deadlines.

### Work a small example

A slow data-access process may be improved with a scoped approved dataset rather than copying unrestricted private data to a convenient location.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Treating speed as justification for ignoring an applicable security or privacy constraint.
