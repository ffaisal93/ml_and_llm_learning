## Question

L6 behavioral conducted by a senior Director. Discussion-style — interviewer probes past leadership stories (tree-level and forest-level) and weaves in policy-vs-efficiency dilemmas that feel like trolley-problem variants. No standard 'tell me about a time' prompts; expect to defend judgments live.

### Requirements

60-minute discussion-style behavioral, not a checklist of standard prompts.
Cover past projects at two levels: project-detail ('the tree') and strategic / cross-team ('the forest').
Be ready for policy-vs-efficiency dilemmas presented as hypotheticals — e.g. 'a new safety policy slows down the simulator pipeline; how do you weigh it against shipping velocity?'
The interviewer is typically a senior D / VP-level engineer; expect direct push-back and follow-up on every claim.
Notes
Structure stories with cause → choice → outcome → reflection. Senior interviewers credit the reflection layer specifically — what would you do differently, what did the experience change in your decision framework.
Tree vs forest. Have two of each kind of story prepared:
Tree-level: concrete engineering decision, with numbers, that you owned end-to-end.
Forest-level: cross-team or organizational decision where you influenced direction without holding the authority.
Policy-vs-efficiency dilemmas. Frame the response around explicit trade-off axes (safety, velocity, cost, fairness, downstream maintenance). Pick a stance and defend it; refusing to commit is a documented red flag at the L6 bar.
Influence without authority. Be ready to describe a specific time you changed another team's roadmap. The interviewer will ask 'what would have happened if you hadn't pushed' — have a credible counterfactual.
Pushback handling. When the interviewer challenges a choice, restate the trade-off in their language before defending. This signals you can update on new information rather than reciting a memorized story.
Don't oversell. Senior interviewers notice puffed-up impact claims; quantify outcomes conservatively and acknowledge collaborators by name.
Specific Waymo themes that have surfaced: decisions weighing rider safety against fleet utilization; ML model promotion gates; managing cross-team review for safety-critical changes; deprecating a beloved feature for compliance reasons.

## Worked solution

### Choose a stance and update it under pressure

No candidate history is provided. Prepare four **real** stories; the following are illustrative rehearsal choices, not personal achievements. Two tree stories might be a model-promotion gate that caught a stale-feature bug and a simulator cache redesign that reduced repeated work. Two forest stories might be agreeing a shared safety-evaluation contract across teams and retiring a popular unmaintainable workflow. For each, record cause→choice→outcome→reflection, your authority boundary, an actual scale/cost number if known, and the collaborators' contributions.

For “a new safety policy slows the simulator,” take a concrete position: keep the required gate, quantify where its time is spent and reduce redundant work without weakening its safety purpose. Separate mandatory high-risk scenario coverage from duplicated parsing/setup; cache only immutable, policy-versioned intermediate results. Parallelize independent scenarios with bounded resources and prioritize release-critical workloads. A changed policy invalidates cached evidence. If a temporary exception is possible, use the designated review authority and time-limited scope; do not unilaterally waive a gate because velocity fell.

An illustrative decision compares a two-day delay to a weakly characterized risk. I would choose the delay while collecting evidence, set an explicit investigation deadline and give stakeholders scope/date options. If pushback reveals the new check is redundant with an independently equivalent validated gate, I would change the implementation after proving equivalence. Safety, schedule, cost, fairness across teams and maintenance are explicit axes; “safety first” without an executable plan is incomplete.

For influence without authority, explain the other team's goal, the shared failure evidence, the small pilot you negotiated and the roadmap trade you helped fund. The counterfactual should be bounded: without the shared contract, the specific stale-version failure would likely have remained undetected, not “the entire organization would have failed.” Report actual outcomes conservatively, including uncertainty and any ongoing operational cost. When challenged, restate the trade-off, name the new evidence and say whether it changes the decision. Reflection should describe a later behavior change, such as agreeing acceptance criteria before implementation, rather than claiming you would make every original choice again.
