## Question

Analyze how ordinary least-squares slopes combine across two disjoint samples, then reuse a fitted model to select and add one new regressor. The goal is to distinguish what can be inferred from fitted slopes alone and to avoid repeatedly fitting the full expanded model.

Given
Sample 1 contains X1 and Y1 with M observations; sample 2 contains X2 and Y2 with N observations. In parts 1 through 3, each X and Y is a single column and both within-sample predictor sums of squares are positive.
In parts 4 and 5, X1 is an N by k full-column-rank matrix, X2 is an N by (f-k) matrix, e is the residual from regressing Y on X1, and X1 = QR is an available thin QR factorization.
Treat M and N as known in part 3, and also state what can be guessed if only the two slopes are known.
Questions
1. Fit without intercepts on both samples and their concatenation. Given the two sample slopes beta1 and beta2, derive the combined slope beta and give its exact possible range, including beta1 = beta2.
2. Repeat with an intercept in every regression. Given only the two within-sample slopes, determine the possible pooled slope. Assume the attainable set has no gaps.
3. Return to regressions without intercepts and assume all (x, y) pairs are i.i.d. from one zero-mean bivariate Gaussian distribution. Give a reasonable estimate of beta using beta1, beta2, M, and N, and state the equal-size or unknown-size approximation.
4. Compute which column of X2 produces the largest reduction in residual sum of squares when added to X1. Derive the reduction formula and give the cost of scanning all candidates using the existing QR factorization.
5. For the selected column, derive the new coefficient and the update to the k existing coefficients without a full refit.
Efficiency target

Do not fit f-k separate augmented models or refit the full design from scratch, which can cost O(N f^2). The candidate scan should use matrix projections in O(N k (f-k)) time, followed by an O(k^2) triangular solve for the coefficient update.

### Constraints

M, N &gt;= 2; 1 &lt;= k &lt; f &lt;= N.
Ignore a candidate column whose component orthogonal to X1 has zero norm, because it adds no identifiable direction.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

#### Pool two disjoint ordinary least-squares datasets correctly.

The pooled solution depends on sufficient statistics, not a simple average of coefficients.

1. For each dataset retain S_i=X_iᵀX_i and t_i=X_iᵀy_i using the same feature and intercept conventions.
2. Sum S=S1+S2 and t=t1+t2, then solve S*beta=t when well-posed, or use stable factorization-based alternatives.
3. Inspect conditioning and rank; for raw data, QR/SVD avoids unnecessary normal-equation instability.

#### Add a feature to an existing least-squares regression efficiently.

Only the part of the new feature unexplained by current features can explain additional response variation.

1. Residualize the new column z against X to get z_perp, and residualize y to get y_perp.
2. If z_perp has nonzero squared norm, its incremental coefficient is z_perpᵀy_perp/(z_perpᵀz_perp).
3. Update old coefficients consistently using the projection of z onto X, or update a QR factorization directly.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Prefer projections and stable solves to memorized inverses

Least squares finds the projection of the response onto the feature space:

$$
\hat\beta=\arg\min_\beta\|X\beta-y\|_2^2,
\qquad X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y.
$$

The normal equations explain the mathematics, but explicitly forming $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is usually a poor numerical implementation. Squaring the condition number can amplify instability. QR or SVD-based least-squares routines expose rank and near-dependence more reliably.

For two datasets with the same feature definitions, sufficient statistics add:

$$
S=X_1^\top X_1+X_2^\top X_2,\qquad
 t=X_1^\top y_1+X_2^\top y_2.
$$

Solving the pooled problem is not equivalent to averaging the two coefficient vectors. Rows carry different directional information, not only different sample counts.

State centering, intercept and regularization conventions. With ridge, the penalized directions satisfy $(X^\top X+\lambda I)\beta=X^\top y$, but an unpenalized intercept needs separate treatment. Distinguish coefficient identification from predictive usefulness: correlated columns can make individual coefficients unstable while their combined predictions remain fairly stable. Validate forecasts on independent data; impressive training fit, including interpolation of noise, is not evidence of a real predictive relation.

#### Put the result in mathematical notation

Pooled normal equations use
$$\hat\beta=(X_1^\top X_1+X_2^\top X_2)^{-1}(X_1^\top y_1+X_2^\top y_2),$$
when the inverse exists; implement with a stable solve/factorization instead. For residualized new feature $z_\perp$ and response $y_\perp$,
$$\hat\gamma=\frac{z_\perp^\top y_\perp}{z_\perp^\top z_\perp}.$$
A zero denominator means no new identifiable direction, not an invitation to divide by a numerical near-zero.

### Work a small example

One dataset with 10 rows and another with 1000 generally should not contribute equally by averaging their coefficient vectors.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Averaging coefficients without their design information or ignoring different feature encodings.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**Add a feature to an existing least-squares regression efficiently.** If z is an exact copy of an existing column, z_perp=0 and it provides no new identifiable direction.

Why is this related to partial regression? Both isolate a predictor's contribution after controlling for other columns.
