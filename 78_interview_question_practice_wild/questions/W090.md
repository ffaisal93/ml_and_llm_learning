## Question

A model you trained for a credit-risk task performs worse than expected. Use the bias-variance trade-off to explain why a model can fail in two opposite ways, how you would tell which one you are facing, and what you would change in each case.

### Requirements

Answer all five parts.
1. Definitions. Define bias and variance of a learned model, and state the decomposition of expected squared prediction error into bias, variance and irreducible noise.
2. Failure examples. Give one concrete example of a high-bias model and one of a high-variance model, and say how each looks in the training error and the validation error.
3. Capacity. Explain how test error behaves as model capacity grows, and where the best model sits on that curve.
4. Diagnosis. Describe the experiments you would run (learning curves, comparing training and validation error, varying capacity or training-set size) to decide whether more data, a richer model, or stronger regularisation is the right fix.
5. Remedies. For each failure type, list at least two changes you would try and explain why each one moves the model along the trade-off, including how L2 regularisation, dropout and early stopping reduce variance at a small cost in bias.
Expected answer

A structured explanation that covers every part, uses the error decomposition correctly, gives one example per failure mode, and ends with a clear decision rule connecting the diagnosis to the remedy.

## Worked solution

### 1. Define the two sources of model error

At a fixed credit profile x, consider models trained on repeated samples D. Bias is the difference between the average prediction and the true conditional mean, while variance is the sensitivity of the fitted prediction to D. For squared error,

$$E[(Y-\hat f_D(x))^2]=(E_D\hat f_D(x)-f(x))^2+
\operatorname{Var}_D(\hat f_D(x))+\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid x).$$

The last term is irreducible given the available features. Credit default is often binary; this squared-error decomposition applies to probability prediction/Brier-style loss, not literally unchanged to classification accuracy or log loss.

### 2–3. Connect failure examples to capacity

A linear score using only income may miss debt-to-income interactions and nonlinear repayment risk. If optimization succeeds but both training and validation quality remain poor, that suggests high bias. A deep tree on a few hundred borrowers can memorize rare feature combinations: excellent training performance, weak validation performance and unstable fits suggest high variance.

In a classical capacity sweep, training error falls with complexity while test error initially falls then rises; the useful region minimizes validation error rather than training error. Modern overparameterized models can have non-U-shaped curves, so use measurements instead of assuming a universal shape. The best point also depends on calibration, fairness, latency and interpretability requirements.

### 4. Diagnose with controlled experiments

First rule out leakage, target parsing bugs and a train/deployment population shift. Split by time with mature default outcomes, and keep related records for the same borrower grouped as appropriate. Compare training and validation performance using the same loss and appropriate risk metrics. Plot both against training-set size and against capacity/regularization, and repeat fits to inspect uncertainty.

If both errors are high and adding data does little, test better features, a richer model or less regularization. If the gap is large and more data shrinks it, prioritize representative data and variance controls. A large gap caused by a new lending policy is distribution shift; blindly reducing model capacity may not repair it.

### 5. Choose the remedy for the evidence

For high bias, add justified nonlinearities/interactions or a more expressive model, and reduce an excessive L2 penalty or an overly short training budget. Check optimization first: an untrained large network can also have high training loss.

For high variance, collect more representative labeled cases, increase regularization, reduce capacity or use bagging. L2 shrinks weights; dropout discourages reliance on a few hidden activations; early stopping limits fitting late-training noise. These often lower variance at some bias cost, but the effect must be validated. Dropout is mainly a neural-network technique and must be disabled during ordinary evaluation.

The decision rule is: poor training **and** validation performance calls for checking representation/optimization and reducing underfitting; good training with substantially worse validation calls for checking leakage/shift, then stabilizing generalization. Select the change by held-out risk quality and calibration, not by an attractive training curve alone.
