## Worked solution

### Optimizers: distinguish direction from coordinate scaling

SGD updates $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta g_t$. Mini-batches estimate the full gradient with noise while exploiting hardware parallelism. Momentum accumulates $m_t=\beta m_{t-1}+(1-\beta)g_t$ and steps along m, damping oscillation and retaining consistent directions. State the convention because some implementations omit the factor $1-\beta$ and rescale the learning rate.

AdaGrad accumulates squared gradients, so frequently updated coordinates receive smaller steps; its learning rates can decay too far. RMSProp uses an exponential second-moment average. Adam combines first/second moments with bias correction:

$$\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon}.$$

AdamW applies weight decay separately from that adaptive update. Momentum helps noisy/ill-conditioned directions, but every optimizer still needs learning-rate/schedule tuning. Adaptive does not mean invariant to every parameterization or guaranteed better test quality.

### Scaling laws: a planning model, not a promise

A useful empirical form for language-model loss is

$$L(N,D)\approx L_\infty+A N^{-a}+B D^{-b},$$

where N is parameter count, D training tokens and positive fitted exponents describe a measured regime. Dense training compute is approximately proportional to ND, with architecture-dependent constants. At fixed compute, spending everything on a larger model leaves fewer training tokens; a compute-efficient allocation balances the marginal loss reduction from both terms.

Fit laws on relevant smaller runs with comparable data/architecture and account for uncertainty. Data quality, repeated tokens, context length, optimization and serving cost can change the optimum. MoE total and active parameters play different roles. Lower pretraining loss does not automatically imply proportional improvement on every downstream product task.

### K-means: hard assignments

The objective is $\sum_i\|x_i-\mu_{z_i}\|^2$. Assign each point to its nearest center, then replace nonempty centers with their assigned means. Each step does not increase the objective, but convergence is generally to a local optimum, not a globally best partition. Initialization, feature scaling, outliers and empty clusters matter; use k-means++/restarts and an explicit empty-cluster policy.

K-means fits roughly spherical equal-scale groups; cluster indices have no inherent meaning. Increasing k always weakly reduces the optimum training objective, so choose k using held-out utility, stability or an appropriate selection diagnostic rather than training inertia alone.

### GMM: soft probabilistic assignments

A Gaussian mixture models $p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\mathcal N(x\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$. EM's E-step computes responsibilities

$$r_{ik}=\frac{\pi_k\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}.$$

The M-step updates mixing weights, means and covariances using these fractional counts. Full covariances represent elliptical clusters; diagonal/shared covariance choices reduce parameters. Use log-sum-exp for numerical stability and covariance regularization to avoid singular collapse. EM improves likelihood toward a local stationary solution and needs careful initialization.

K-means resembles the hard-assignment/small equal-isotropic-variance limit of a mixture, but a general GMM learns overlap and cluster shape. GMM likelihood is a density objective, not automatically a better semantic clustering metric. In LLM practice, the same discipline applies: define the objective, inspect optimization behavior and validate the intended downstream use rather than treating a familiar algorithm as universally appropriate.
