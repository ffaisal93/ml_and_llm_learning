## Worked solution

### Solution outline

#### Explain logistic regression from scores to gradients.

Logistic regression models binary probability with a linear score passed through a sigmoid.

1. Compute z=xᵀw+b and p=sigmoid(z); p/(1-p)=exp(z), so the log odds are linear.
2. Fit weights by minimizing binary cross-entropy, preferably calculated from logits for stability.
3. The score gradient is p-y, giving weight gradient Xᵀ(p-y)/N under mean reduction. Choose the classification threshold using validation costs and metrics.

#### Diagnose high bias versus high variance.

Use training and validation behavior to distinguish insufficient fitting from poor generalization.

1. If both errors remain high, check the pipeline and optimization first, then consider inadequate features or capacity.
2. If training error is low but validation error is high, check leakage-free splitting, distribution shift and overfitting.
3. Use learning curves and controlled changes: more data, regularization, simpler models or better features depending on the diagnosis.

#### Compare L1 and L2 regularization.

Both penalize large coefficients, but their geometry produces different coefficient patterns.

1. Add $\lambda\lVert w\rVert_1$ for L1 or a stated lambda-scaled squared norm for L2.
2. L1 can produce exact zeros with suitable optimization, aiding sparse selection; L2 usually shrinks coefficients smoothly and stabilizes correlated predictors.
3. Standardize features using training statistics, tune lambda on validation data and state whether the intercept is penalized.

#### Compare random forests and gradient boosting.

Random forests average diversified trees; boosting adds models sequentially to improve an objective.

1. A forest typically trains trees on bootstrapped samples with feature randomness, reducing variance through averaging.
2. Boosting fits successive updates informed by current errors or loss gradients; learning rate, tree depth and stopping determine its capacity.
3. Compare time-aware validation, calibration, inference cost and sensitivity to hyperparameters rather than selecting by reputation.

#### Why use AdamW, warmup and gradient clipping?

They address different training problems: adaptive update scale, early-step instability and unusually large gradients.

1. Adam maintains first and second moment estimates, with bias correction after zero initialization.
2. AdamW applies decoupled weight decay rather than treating decay exactly as another adaptively scaled loss gradient.
3. Warm up learning rate over initial steps and clip the global gradient norm when appropriate. Inspect logs and ablations; these are tuning tools, not correctness fixes.

#### Implement k-means with convergence and empty clusters.

Alternate assigning points to their closest centers and moving each center to the mean of its assigned points.

1. Initialize k distinct data indices under an explicit random seed, requiring 1<=k<=N.
2. Compute squared distances, assign closest centers and recompute non-empty means. For an empty cluster, retain its center or explicitly reseed it.
3. Stop on a center-shift tolerance or iteration cap, then recompute labels for the returned centers. Compare objective values, not center ordering, across implementations.
4. For GMM, alternate log-space responsibility normalization and weighted updates of mixture weights, means and regularized covariances; stop by likelihood change and inspect empty/collapsed components.
5. For scaling laws, write a fitted loss model such as L(N,D)=L_infinity+A*N^-alpha+B*D^-beta under an explicitly measured regime; choose N and D under a compute budget and validate rather than treating a fitted exponent as a universal constant.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Why one penalty creates zeros and the other usually does not

State the loss convention before comparing penalty coefficients. With a mean data loss, one possible pair of objectives is

$$
J_1(w)=\frac1N\sum_i\ell_i(w)+\lambda\|w\|_1,
\qquad
J_2(w)=\frac1N\sum_i\ell_i(w)+\frac\lambda2\|w\|_2^2.
$$

L2 contributes derivative $\lambda w$, continuously shrinking larger coefficients. L1 has subgradient $\lambda\operatorname{sign}(w_j)$ away from zero and an interval $[-\lambda,\lambda]$ at zero. That interval can balance the data gradient while the coefficient remains **exactly zero**.

A one-coordinate example makes the distinction concrete. For the quadratic data term $\tfrac12(w-z)^2$, the solutions are

$$
w_{\rm L1}=\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,0),\qquad
w_{\rm L2}=\frac{z}{1+\lambda}.
$$

With $z=0.8$ and $\lambda=1$, L1 gives zero and L2 gives $0.4$. These closed forms describe this specific separable objective; arbitrary correlated-design regression is not solved by independently applying them to the raw targets.

Scale features using training-only statistics, otherwise coefficient magnitude reflects units as well as influence. Usually state separately whether the intercept is penalized. For correlated predictors, L1 can choose one somewhat arbitrarily, whereas L2 often distributes weight and stabilizes predictions. Neither sparse selection nor a large coefficient establishes causality.

**When to use.** L1 is useful when sparsity itself helps interpretation or deployment; L2 is a strong stability baseline. Tune the penalty within the validation procedure and compare the actual predictive/operational objective. Elastic net combines both penalties rather than claiming one is universally superior.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**Diagnose high bias versus high variance.** Training error 1% and validation error 25% suggests a generalization problem, but a group-distribution mismatch could be the cause rather than model complexity alone.

Why can a larger model generalize better? Optimization and inductive bias can change along with capacity; simple monotonic stories are incomplete.

**Compare L1 and L2 regularization.** With two almost identical predictors, L1 may choose one unstably while L2 spreads weight across both.

What is elastic net? A combination that can encourage sparsity while stabilizing correlated groups.

**Compare random forests and gradient boosting.** A hundred independent high-variance trees can become stable when their errors are weakly correlated; a hundred almost identical trees average away much less error.

Why do forests often need less tuning? Their averaging is relatively robust, though feature leakage and distribution shift still matter.

**Why use AdamW, warmup and gradient clipping?** A gradient [3,4] has norm 5. Clipping its norm to 1 yields [0.6,0.8], preserving direction before the optimizer's own transformation.

Why unscale mixed-precision gradients before clipping? Otherwise the threshold is applied to artificially scaled gradients.
