## Question

Let S be symmetric positive definite and v0 nonzero. Define v_(i+1)=S*v_i/||S*v_i||². Analyze direction, even/odd norms, convergence, scaling, repeated top eigenvalues and nonzero fixed-point stability. Compare with the ordinary unit-normalized power method; treat zero top-eigenspace projection separately.

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

1. Write v_i=r_i*u_i with unit u_i. Then u_(i+1)=S*u_i/||S*u_i|| and r_(i+1)=1/(r_i*||S*u_i||). Direction follows the ordinary power method; a nonzero top-eigenspace projection is almost sure for a continuous initialization.
2. With a simple top eigenvalue, u_i tends to the top eigenvector with the sign of its initial coefficient. Let a_i=||S*u_i||→lambda_1; then r_(i+2)/r_i=a_i/a_(i+1). The exponentially convergent directional ratios imply positive even/odd radial limits A and B with A*B=1/lambda_1.
3. Thus vectors generally approach a two-cycle, converging only when A=B=1/sqrt(lambda_1). This remains true when lambda_1<1; it does not imply norms vanish. A repeated top eigenvalue yields the normalized initial projection onto that eigenspace, barring zero-projection exceptions.
4. T(c*v)=T(v)/c: even iterates scale by c and odd iterates by 1/c, so this is not scale-invariant in the prompt’s sense. Fixed points are eigenvectors of eigenvalue lambda with norm 1/sqrt(lambda); radial derivative is -1 and transverse eigen-directions scale by lambda_j/lambda. Top directions are transversely stable/neutral, lower eigenspaces have unstable higher-eigenvalue directions.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Prefer projections and stable solves to memorized inverses

Least squares finds the projection of the response onto the feature space:

$$
\hat\beta=\arg\min_\beta\|X\beta-y\|_2^2,
\qquad X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y.
$$

The normal equations explain the mathematics, but explicitly forming $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ is usually a poor numerical implementation. Squaring the condition number can amplify instability. QR or SVD-based least-squares routines expose rank and near-dependence more reliably.

For two datasets with the same feature definitions, sufficient statistics add:

$$
S=X_1^\top X_1+X_2^\top X_2,\qquad
 t=X_1^\top y_1+X_2^\top y_2.
$$

Solving the pooled problem is not equivalent to averaging the two coefficient vectors. Rows carry different directional information, not only different sample counts.

State centering, intercept and regularization conventions. With ridge, the penalized directions satisfy $(X^\top X+\lambda I)\beta=X^\top y$, but an unpenalized intercept needs separate treatment. Distinguish coefficient identification from predictive usefulness: correlated columns can make individual coefficients unstable while their combined predictions remain fairly stable. Validate forecasts on independent data; impressive training fit, including interpolation of noise, is not evidence of a real predictive relation.

#### Put the result in mathematical notation

With $v_i=r_i u_i$ and $\|u_i\|=1$,
$$u_{i+1}=\frac{Su_i}{\|Su_i\|},\qquad
r_{i+1}=\frac1{r_i\|Su_i\|},\qquad
\frac{r_{i+2}}{r_i}=\frac{\|Su_i\|}{\|Su_{i+1}\|}.$$
When the direction approaches the top eigenspace, positive even/odd norm limits satisfy $AB=1/\lambda_1$. Convergence requires $A=B=1/\sqrt{\lambda_1}$. Scaling is alternating: $T(cv)=T(v)/c$, not the ordinary homogeneous scale rule.

### Work a small example

For S=[4] and v0=1, the sequence alternates 1 and 1/4; its direction is already constant but the vector does not converge. v0=1/2 is fixed.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Mistaking convergence of direction for convergence of the full vector, or analyzing division by ||Sv|| instead of its square.
