## Question

This problem has four parts on the data behind a machine-learning project: where it comes from, whether it may be used, how it is cleaned, and how to keep evaluation honest.

Task

Choose one machine-learning project you have built and answer the following.

Part 1 — Provenance and labels

For your project, state:
1. The source of the data (collected, purchased, scraped, generated by a model, or inherited), its volume, and the time window it covers.
2. Your right to use it: licence or terms of service, consent, and how personal data was handled.
3. Who produced the labels, against what guideline, and how label quality was measured (for example inter-annotator agreement or an audited sample), with the numbers.
Part 2 — Cleaning and splitting
1. Describe how you removed exact duplicates and near-duplicates, and how many examples each step removed.
2. Specify how you split train, validation and test, and by which unit (row, user, document, session, or time). Justify the unit.
Part 3 — Augmentation leakage (quantitative)

A dataset of N base examples is augmented into K near-identical copies per example, giving N·K rows. The rows are then split at random, with a fraction f of them going to test.
1. Derive, as a function of K and f, the probability that a test row has at least one sibling (a copy of the same base example) in the training set. Evaluate it for K = 5 and f = 0.2.
2. Suppose every base example carries a random binary label, so no model can truly exceed 50% accuracy. What accuracy would a 1-nearest-neighbour model report on this split, and why?
3. Give the correct procedure, and state where augmentation belongs relative to the split.
Part 4 — Contamination and scale
1. Describe how you would detect overlap between a training corpus and a public evaluation set, including the matching method and its window size.
2. If the dataset had to grow 100×, which part of the pipeline would break first, what would you automate, and what would you keep under human review?

## Worked solution

### Solution outline

#### Discuss the data strategy behind an ML project.

Data choices can explain more of a project's outcome than the architecture name.

1. Describe collection, permissions, label generation, cleaning and the intended deployment distribution.
2. Explain split design, duplicates, hard examples, reviewer quality and versioning.
3. Connect one data intervention to a controlled result and identify unresolved gaps.

#### Audit human-labeled training data before scaling it.

More labels help only if they carry the intended signal and match the task definition.

1. Inspect the sampling frame, instructions, class balance, duplicates, annotator disagreement and gold-label checks.
2. Separate ambiguous examples from clear errors; compare disagreement across languages, subgroups and content types.
3. Run a controlled data-quality intervention against a fixed model and evaluation set before attributing improvements to architecture.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Establish what the number actually estimates

Write the population, unit of analysis, outcome window and denominator next to the metric. A per-user mean and a per-impression mean answer different questions. A global mean over unequal batches is

$$
\bar\ell=\frac{\sum_b\sum_{i\in b}\ell_i}{\sum_b n_b},
$$

not generally the unweighted mean of batch means. Workers should reduce numerators and denominators separately when reporting that metric.

Use a comparison with matched examples, mature labels and unchanged definitions. For uncertainty, resample independent units; repeated events within a user or prompt are not automatically independent. A confidence interval measures sampling uncertainty under assumptions, not protection against leaked data or an inappropriate target.

Calibration asks whether predictions around probability $p$ are correct about fraction $p$ of the time. It is not the same as accuracy. A reliability plot should show bin populations, and a calibration-error formula must say whether bins or examples receive equal weight.

When quality regresses, trace data availability, feature freshness, label delays and cohort mix before automatically retraining. For example, a fraud model may look worse on immature labels even when its true behavior has not changed. Report missing/time-out outputs explicitly: dropping them from the denominator can turn an unreliable system into an apparently perfect one.

### Work a small example

Improving annotation guidelines while holding the model fixed can show a clearer causal contribution than changing data and architecture together.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Calling the dataset “clean” without saying what was checked.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**Audit human-labeled training data before scaling it.** Two annotators can disagree because the policy is unclear, not because one is a spammer; rewriting the rubric may help more than filtering a rater.

How would you prioritize a review budget? Combine uncertainty, business cost, diversity and suspected systematic errors.
