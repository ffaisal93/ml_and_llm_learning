## Worked solution

### Solution outline

1. Linked lists store nodes with references: sequential access $O(n)$, $O(1)$ insertion/deletion once the relevant node/predecessor is known; arrays offer $O(1)$ indexed access and shifting costs for interior insertion. Quicksort partitions around a pivot: expected $O(n \log n)$ with suitable randomization, worst $O(n^2)$, with recursion-space caveats.
2. Compilation translates code before or during execution; interpretation executes a representation at runtime. Real runtimes can mix interpretation, bytecode and JIT. Newton solves f(x)=0 with x_next=x-f/f_prime; near a simple root it can converge quadratically, but small derivatives or poor starts can fail.
3. A square matrix is invertible iff full rank; solve via pivoted factorization rather than explicitly forming an inverse for Ax=b. A Jacobian holds partial derivatives J_ij=partial output_i/partial input_j. Backprop applies products/vector-Jacobian products in reverse graph order.
4. Bayes: P(A|B)=P(B|A)P(A)/P(B), for nonzero P(B). Inverse-CDF sampling uses F_inverse(U), U uniform. Variance expands to E[X²]-E[X]² when the second moment exists.
5. An SVM maximizes a margin with hinge-loss/slack tradeoffs; kernels replace inner products with a valid kernel representation. High dimensions increase data requirements and can weaken distance discrimination; regularization, representation and validation matter.

### Understand why the approach works

#### Explain the assumptions behind a familiar formula

A definition, implementation and asymptotic guarantee are related but different. A linked list can insert in constant time **given the needed node/predecessor**, but locating that node may take linear time. Randomized quicksort has expected $O(n\log n)$ comparisons, not a universal worst-case guarantee.

For numerical mathematics, write the function and the local approximation. Newton's method comes from setting the tangent-line estimate to zero:

$$
0\approx f(x_k)+f'(x_k)(x_{k+1}-x_k),\qquad
x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}.
$$

A simple root and a sufficiently good starting point can give quadratic local convergence; small derivatives or a poor start can cause failure. A memorized update alone does not explain that behavior.

For probability limits, state moment and independence/dependence assumptions. The CLT concerns a normalized sample mean, not a claim that all raw data become normally distributed. For information quantities, state support and log base. A useful interview answer gives the definition, one small example, the computational or statistical consequence and one case where the usual shortcut is invalid.

### Work a small example

For f(x)=x²-2, Newton’s update is (x+2/x)/2; starting at 1 produces 1.5 then about 1.4167. This illustrates local improvement without proving global convergence.

### Edge cases and common mistakes

Giving $O(1)$ linked-list search or claiming every runtime is exclusively compiled or interpreted.

### Check your reasoning on related cases

**How do you gradient-check a new numerical layer?** If analytic gradient is 0.25 and the central difference is 0.25000001, the difference is consistent with ordinary floating-point error.

Why not use an arbitrarily tiny h? Subtractive cancellation eventually overwhelms the signal.

**Use Bayes' rule for a rare-event detector.** With prevalence 1%, sensitivity 90% and false-positive rate 5%, precision is 0.009/(0.009+0.0495), about 15.4%.

What if prevalence rises? Precision rises under unchanged sensitivity and false-positive rate, though real conditional rates may also change.

**Explain multicollinearity and stable least squares.** If one column is exactly twice another, several coefficient pairs produce the same predictions. The individual coefficients are not identifiable without an extra convention.

Does dropping one correlated variable always improve test prediction? No; compare held-out performance and the intended interpretation.
